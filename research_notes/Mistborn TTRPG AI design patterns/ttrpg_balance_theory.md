# TTRPG Balance Theory: Named Techniques for Character Build Diversity vs. Mechanical Balance

## How does D&D 5e's "bounded accuracy" work, and what problem was it designed to solve vs. 3.x/4e? What are its known limitations?

### Takeaway
Bounded accuracy is 5e's core design philosophy of capping how far numeric bonuses (attack, AC, DC, save, skill) can climb with level, so that the d20's natural spread (1–20) always matters and low-level threats/items stay relevant at high level. It solved 3.x/4e's "numbers escalation" problem but is widely blamed for enabling (not preventing) the martial/caster power disparity and for "trap option" feats, and that disparity remains a live, unresolved debate even after the 2024 PHB revision.

### Cited Findings
- Bounded accuracy is "a design principle in Dungeons & Dragons 5th edition which limits the numeric bonuses to d20-based rolls which accrue with character level" and is not written as an explicit rule in the PHB but is the foundational philosophy of the edition — [DnD Lore Wiki](https://dungeonsdragons.fandom.com/wiki/Bounded_accuracy)
- Specific caps: level-based bonus to attacks/saves/skills capped around +6 (proficiency bonus), magic weapon bonuses capped at +3, ability scores capped at 20 (max +5 modifier), max DC of 30, max AC of 30, giving a theoretical max total bonus around +11; feats that grant flat numeric attack bonuses were removed in favor of advantage for favorable circumstances — [DnD Lore Wiki](https://dungeonsdragons.fandom.com/wiki/Bounded_accuracy)
- Purpose: keep low-level monsters mechanically relevant at high levels (attack bonus and AC don't get wildly outpaced), which let 5e eliminate the 4e "minion" monster category and let large groups of ordinary enemies (e.g., an orc horde) remain a credible threat to high-level parties — [DnD Lore Wiki](https://dungeonsdragons.fandom.com/wiki/Bounded_accuracy)
- With a realistic max modifier around +11, "the result of a d20 almost always matters," in contrast to earlier editions where modifiers grew so large the die roll became comparatively insignificant — [Blog of Heroes](https://hill-kleerup.org/blog/heroes/2022/09/dd-5e-rules-bounded-accuracy.html); [Study Hall #6](http://www.adamusdrakeproductions.com/gms-corner/study-hall-6-bounded-accuracy-the-design-philosophy-of-5e)
- On multiclassing balance: a multiclass or "MAD" (Multiple Ability Dependent) character is expected to pay a price of slower primary-stat progression in exchange for versatility; removing that price (e.g., via easy stat-boosting bonuses) is seen by commentators as making the game "less good" because it collapses the specialization/versatility trade-off bounded accuracy depends on — [GiantITP forum discussion](https://forums.giantitp.com/showthread.php?676565-Bounded-accuracy=&p=26185534)
- Content (feats, subclasses, magic items) that lets a build's attack bonus or AC exceed the intended bounded-accuracy envelope is explicitly flagged as breaking the system, since bounded accuracy's monster math assumes PC bonuses stay within its numeric bounds — [GiantITP forum discussion](https://forums.giantitp.com/showthread.php?676565-Bounded-accuracy=&p=26185534)
- The martial/caster disparity ("linear fighter, quadratic wizard") predates 5e but persists under bounded accuracy: fighters "swing slightly harder at high levels" while wizards "reshape reality," leaving martial players feeling like "sidekicks to magic users" — [Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/dnd/are-martial-classes-worth-it)
- The 2024/2025 Player's Handbook revision was explicitly a response to this fever-pitch debate; it made martials "more diverse and viable... than since 4th edition" via universal features like Weapon Mastery and more unique subclass options, but critics say "the core issue remains — martial classes can do cooler stuff now, but they still need to do more for the same results" — [Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/dnd/are-martial-classes-worth-it)
- At high level, casters are treated as "essential" (spells like *Fabricate* let them bypass the gold economy) while martials are "optional," and commentators argue "these gaps are likely unsolvable without redefining D&D's magic-versus-mundane design principles" — [Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/dnd/are-martial-classes-worth-it)

### Inferences
- Bounded accuracy is a technique for keeping the *math* legible and stable across level range (good for build diversity because a wide variety of builds stay "in the same numeric lane"), but it does not by itself solve *capability* disparity — a martial and a caster can have identical to-hit math while one's action economy grants reality-altering utility the other's doesn't. This suggests that for an AI-run system, capping numeric spread is necessary but not sufficient; capability/utility parity requires separate design levers (e.g., spotlight balance, action economy limits — see PF2 below).
- The "price" logic for multiclassing (slower primary progression in exchange for versatility) is a generalizable balancing lever: any "endless customizability" system likely needs an explicit cost axis (e.g., action-economy tax, resource tax, delayed power curve) attached to horizontal expansion of options, or optimization will erode the intended trade-off.

### Gaps
- Could not find a single canonical WotC/Mearls-authored essay explicitly naming "trap options" as a 5e-era design term (see trap-options section below for what was found instead).

## How do Pathfinder 2e's three-action economy and "degrees of success" work, and how does its math-first design philosophy differ from 5e's improvisational approach? Is it more or less friendly to build diversity?

### Takeaway
PF2e replaces 5e's move/standard/bonus action split with a flat pool of 3 actions + 1 reaction per turn, and replaces binary pass/fail with four "degrees of success" (critical success/success/failure/critical failure), determined by how far a roll beats or misses the DC (beat by 10+ = critical success, miss by 10+ = critical failure; a natural 20/1 shifts the degree by one step). Design commentary frames PF2e as "math-first" (tight numerical guidance for GMs, explicit encounter/DC math) versus 5e's "rulings over rules" improvisational philosophy — and this precision is explicitly credited with eliminating "trap options" and enabling flavor-driven picks without mechanical punishment, though critics say the tight math makes individual bonuses feel less impactful.

### Cited Findings
- "Pathfinder Second Edition gives every character in an encounter three actions during their turn... and one reaction," enabling more dynamic battlefield movement, efficient attacking, and choices between defense and offense — [Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- Common action costs: Stride (move) = 1 action; Strike (attack) = 1 action; Raise a Shield = 1 action; Cast a Spell usually = 2 actions; Opening a door = 1 action — [Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- The four degrees of success are Critical Failure, Failure, Success, and Critical Success; beating the DC by 10+ upgrades a success to a critical success (and missing by 10+ downgrades to critical failure); a natural 20 improves the degree of success by one step, a natural 1 worsens it by one step — [Archives of Nethys / search synthesis](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=1026); [Pathfinder Authority](https://pathfinderauthority.com/pathfinder-critical-hits-and-success-degrees)
- This four-tier system "replaces the patchwork of special-case rules from earlier editions with four defined outcome tiers that govern every check in the game" — [search synthesis of Nethys/Pathfinder Authority sources]
- Design-philosophy contrast: PF2e "asks the GM to understand more rules, but it also gives more structure, with encounter math, conditions, actions, treasure, DCs, and monster design having clearer support," whereas 5e embraces "rulings over rules" — when situations lack clear rules, the GM makes a quick decision and play continues — reducing prep time but requiring GM comfort with improvisation — [DnD Dice Roller Blog](https://www.dnddiceroller.com/blog/pathfinder-2e-vs-dnd-5e/)
- PF2e is "generally more tactical," where "positioning matters, conditions matter, teamwork matters, a +1 bonus matters," and choosing between attacking again, raising a shield, moving, demoralizing, tripping, recalling knowledge, or casting a spell "can matter a lot" — [DnD Dice Roller Blog](https://www.dnddiceroller.com/blog/pathfinder-2e-vs-dnd-5e/)
- Net framing: "D&D 5E asks for more judgement calls, while Pathfinder 2e asks for more rules fluency" — [DnD Dice Roller Blog](https://www.dnddiceroller.com/blog/pathfinder-2e-vs-dnd-5e/)
- On build diversity specifically: PF2e uses "fiddly math-iness to ensure no build is better than any other build by too much of a margin" (e.g., different two-weapon-fighting feats gated to different classes to preserve balance) — [EN World forum synthesis](https://www.enworld.org/threads/regarding-the-complexity-of-pathfinder-2.675063/)
- Supporters argue PF2e offers players "no lack of choice when building characters and no trap options, so you still get a functional character even if you pick feats for flavor" — [search synthesis, Lawful Good Rogue / EN World discussion](https://lawfulgoodrogue.com/2022/04/04/pathfinder-2e-and-an-uncharacteristic-review/)
- Critics counter that the finely-tuned math means "bonuses feel less impactful," with "many people finding the bonuses not feeling meaningful at every level of play" — [search synthesis, EN World/community discussion](https://www.enworld.org/threads/regarding-the-complexity-of-pathfinder-2.675063/)

### Inferences
- PF2e's degrees-of-success mechanic is a generalizable technique for an AI GM: it turns "did it work?" into a deterministic four-way branch computed purely from (roll + modifiers) vs. DC, which is much easier for software to adjudicate consistently than 5e-style DM improvisation on marginal successes.
- The 3-action economy is a technique for bounding *tactical* build diversity: because every character (regardless of build) draws from the same fixed action pool, build diversity is pushed into *what fills the actions* (feats/spells) rather than *how many actions you get*, which prevents action-economy-stacking builds from breaking the game — a useful pattern for an AI-run system that wants exotic builds without needing to hand-verify action-count edge cases.
- The tension "no trap options, but bonuses feel small" suggests a direct dial: tightening math to guarantee viability of all builds trades away the feeling of raw power growth. A "endless customizability" AI RPG will likely face this same dial and should decide deliberately where to sit on it.

### Gaps
- No definitive, citable "is PF2e considered more or less friendly to build diversity than 5e" consensus verdict was found — sources are split (math rigor enables safe diversity vs. math rigor constrains individuality/creative freedom). This is presented above as a genuine open tension rather than resolved fact.

## How do point-buy systems like GURPS handle balance — tradeoffs of a shared point pool vs. class/level-gated systems?

### Takeaway
GURPS uses a single shared character-point pool spent on attributes, advantages, skills, and (in reverse) refunded by taking disadvantages; the designers' own framing is that points create *identity*, not *balance* — raw point-total parity does not guarantee combat or narrative parity, and the system leans on GM enforcement (of disadvantages, of point limits) rather than structural class/level gating to keep things fair.

### Cited Findings
- GURPS "uses a point-based character creation system where characters are represented by four basic stats (Strength, Dexterity, IQ and Health), and players can buy any number of advantages, disadvantages, perks, quirks and skills" — [GURPS Wiki, search synthesis](https://gurps.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_A_Character)
- Explicit design claim: "the points hadn't created balance—they had created identity"; every character-creation choice "echoes through gameplay," so a character built with no social skills struggles in negotiation while one built with no survival skills struggles in the wilderness — [Kate Plays / GURPS 4th Revised](https://kateplays.substack.com/p/gurps-4th-revised)
- "The total amount of points a character has is not half as important as where and how those points are spent" — illustrated with the example that a 200-point physicist (IQ 20, top scientific skills) "can be chopped into kindling by a 40-point combat-optimized fighter" — [Kate Plays / GURPS 4th Revised](https://kateplays.substack.com/p/gurps-4th-revised)
- Disadvantages ("traits that hinder the character's progress") are taken to refund points into the pool (e.g., a -10-point disadvantage adds 10 points back); GMs are advised to cap disadvantage points at roughly 50% of the starting pool (e.g., -75 points max in a 150-point game) — [search synthesis of SJ Games/GURPS Wiki sources]
- Balance failure mode — min-maxing disadvantages: "GURPS is very easy to min-max in unless the GM sets firm limits on what options are available to the players," and advantage/disadvantage systems are "often criticized for allowing or even encouraging min-maxing," where players "take disadvantages which have little or no tangible effect on play" purely to fund powerful advantages — [GiantITP forum](https://forums.giantitp.com/showthread.php?p=25430690); [TV Tropes / Min-Maxing](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/MinMaxing)
- Specific exploit pattern: buying a flat +1 to IQ (typically 10–20 points) raises *all* IQ-based skills by +1 simultaneously, which is far cheaper than raising each skill individually — a structural stat/skill interaction that rewards min-maxing attribute purchases over skill purchases — [Cannibal Halfling Gaming, "System Hack: GURPS Disadvantages"](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2024/02/14/system-hack-gurps-disadvantages/)
- Structural balance failure: "In GURPS, where the quantity and scope of disadvantages is so broad, it takes an eagle eyed GM to make sure that each disadvantage is 'worth' the point cost," typically enforced by GM roleplay pressure at the table ("negative reinforcement") rather than by the ruleset itself — [Cannibal Halfling Gaming](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2024/02/14/system-hack-gurps-disadvantages/)
- General verdict: "Characters are also theoretically balanced by their point total, but this is only really true in fairly narrow circumstances" — [Cannibal Halfling Gaming](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2024/02/14/system-hack-gurps-disadvantages/)

### Inferences
- Point-buy's core tradeoff versus class/level-gating: it maximizes build-space (any combination is theoretically legal) but pushes the entire balance burden onto (a) the accuracy of individual point-costs relative to actual in-play value, and (b) continuous human/GM judgment to police disadvantage abuse and cross-domain power comparisons ("wizard vs. fighter in a fight" problem). Class/level-gated systems (5e, PF2e) sacrifice some build-space flexibility in exchange for the designer being able to pre-bundle "known-balanced" packages.
- For an AI GM, GURPS's failure mode is instructive: a shared point-pool system is unsafe to run unsupervised unless every advantage/disadvantage/skill has a *rigorously audited, context-independent* point cost, or the AI has some other way (e.g., dynamic disadvantage enforcement, hard caps on stat-to-skill point ratios) to replace the "eagle-eyed GM" who normally polices abuse.

### Gaps
- Could not find a single authoritative post-mortem specifically quantifying how *often* GURPS point-buy games experience these balance failures in practice (findings above are qualitative/forum-sourced, not statistical).

## How do "Powered by the Apocalypse" (PbtA) games structure "moves," and how does that create distinctiveness differently from a stat-and-skill system?

### Takeaway
PbtA replaces open-ended skill checks with a curated list of named "moves" (2d6 + modifier; 10+ = full success, 7–9 = partial success/hard choice, 6- = GM makes a move), and each playbook (character class) gets its own small, hand-authored set of moves — meaning distinctiveness comes from *which narrow, author-curated action-verbs a character has access to* rather than from a shared universal skill list with different numeric ratings.

### Cited Findings
- PbtA games are "typically centered on resolving what characters do as 'moves,'" usually resolved via 2d6 + relevant modifier — [search synthesis of Wikipedia/lumpley games sources]
- Result bands: "10+ are successes, while 7 to 9 are partial successes or hard choices, and results of 6 or less allow the MC to make a move"; the design philosophy deliberately makes the mixed/partial result (7–9) the statistically most common outcome, so "the player's goal is achieved, but some complication or cost arises as well" — [search synthesis]
- Playbooks (the PbtA term for classes) "usually provide all of the options for your specific character type on one page. You don't choose from a big list: you choose a concept, and then select options from that concept" — [theresattrpgforthat.tumblr.com](https://theresattrpgforthat.tumblr.com/post/743574214628474880/system-overview-powered-by-the-apocalypse-pbta)
- "PbtA isn't a system you can adapt to different genres like GURPS, d20, or Fate. It's an approach, a framework, a vocabulary for designing new systems that work how you want them to work" — [StartPlaying blog](https://startplaying.games/blog/posts/what-is-powered-by-the-apocalypse)
- Core design tenet: "failure should still move the plot forward" — the 6- result isn't a dead end but a GM-authored complication — [search synthesis]

### Inferences
- PbtA's distinctiveness mechanism is a useful contrast to point-buy/class-feat systems: instead of *quantitative* differentiation (bigger numbers, more options from a shared pool), it uses *qualitative/narrative* differentiation (unique named verbs bound to fictional triggers). This sidesteps a lot of numeric-balance work (there's no "DPS math" to break) but means the "endless customizability" a player wants must come from re-flavoring/re-combining a deliberately small, hand-authored move list — this is nearly the opposite lever from point-buy's "buy anything," and may be a safer pattern for an AI GM because each move's trigger and resolution is fully pre-specified by the designer (low adjudication ambiguity) at the cost of a smaller total build space.

### Gaps
- Did not find citable material specifically on PbtA "balance" discourse (e.g., documented cases of one playbook being mechanically dominant) — most available sources describe the mechanic itself rather than its balance failure modes. This is a gap for the report writer: PbtA's design goals are explicitly more about narrative pacing than competitive/mechanical balance, so a like-for-like "PbtA trap options" search returned nothing.

## What is "encounter balance math" (CR, XP budgets) and what are its documented failure points?

### Takeaway
5e's Challenge Rating (CR) and XP-budget systems attempt to let a GM predict encounter difficulty from monster stat blocks and party level/size, but are widely and specifically documented as breaking down past low levels because they ignore action economy, monster-count multipliers relative to party size, and non-standard party compositions (summons/allies) — prompting community-made "CR 2.0" style corrections and general advice to combine XP budgets with CR rather than trust either alone.

### Cited Findings
- "The CR system doesn't take most things into consideration so that it isn't even functional as a starting point after a certain level. After level 3, when your players get exponentially more power as they develop, the discrepancy with the CR system gets worse and worse" — [Table Games Hub / community synthesis](https://tablegameshub.com/d-d-challenge-rating-calculator/)
- Concrete documented failure case: "Four characters at the fifth level sometimes can easily beat a monster of CR 9 or even 10, yet die because of several creatures of CR 1," which the source attributes to the system failing to account for action economy and monster composition — [search synthesis, D&D Beyond forums](https://www.dndbeyond.com/forums/dungeons-dragons-discussion/dungeon-masters-only/26409-is-cr-broken-for-building-encounters-my-players)
- Structural gap: "There's no Encounter Size Multiplier table for PCs," causing balance problems when comparing a table of five PCs vs. four vs. three, and further issues "when the PCs have summoned monsters or friendly NPCs on their side" — [search synthesis]
- Official/community acknowledgment: "CR is not entirely precise. It only gives a rough idea about the combat trouble... An improved method for building better encounters, called Challenge Rating 2.0, was born after studies showed important flaws in the original version" — [search synthesis, Table Games Hub sources]
- Practical mitigation advice found in the discourse: "Combining XP budgets with CR, instead of depending only on CR, helps for creating fair fights" — [search synthesis]
- A dedicated critical essay exists specifically arguing 3rd-edition-style Challenge Ratings are fundamentally broken ("3e: Challenge Ratings Are Hot Garbage") — [press.invincible.ink](https://press.invincible.ink/3e-challenge-ratings-are-hot-garbage/) (title/existence confirmed via search; full content not fetched)

### Inferences
- The CR/XP-budget failure pattern (ignoring action economy and party-size scaling) is the encounter-design analog of the character-build balance problem: both stem from trying to compress a multidimensional design space (monster count × action economy × party composition × level) into a single scalar rating. For an AI GM, this suggests CR-style single-number budgets are a risky sole mechanism for "endless customizability" content (e.g., player-created monsters or abilities) — a simulation-based or multi-axis check (e.g., simulated action economy, expected damage-per-round math) would likely be more robust than porting 5e's CR formula directly.

### Gaps
- Did not fetch the full "Challenge Ratings Are Hot Garbage" essay or a primary WotC source explicitly detailing the official "Challenge Rating 2.0"/XP budget math revisions (e.g., the 2014 DMG's "adjusted XP" multiplier table) — flagged here as a gap; the report writer may want a follow-up fetch of press.invincible.ink and the DMG's official multiplier-table language if more mechanical precision is needed.

## Is there a recognized concept of "trap options" in TTRPG design discourse, and what techniques avoid creating them?

### Takeaway
"Trap option" is a recognized, informally-defined term in TTRPG design discourse (most associated with D&D 3rd/4th edition feat design) for a choice that looks appealing but is mechanically weak enough that experienced players learn to avoid it, effectively "punishing" less experienced players who pick it. PF2e's math-first, tightly-audited design (see above) is explicitly cited by its proponents as a technique that eliminates trap options; no single canonical essay/blog post coining the term was found, but the concept is discussed convergently across multiple sources.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "A 'trap option' refers to a mechanic purposefully or accidentally designed to look beneficial to the player but doesn't provide appropriate benefit," particularly associated with D&D 3rd Edition feats, many of which "actually provide little to no benefit," leading experienced players to treat them as "too risky and a trap/waste of experience" — [search synthesis across gamedev/D&D forum sources]
- PF2e is positioned by its defenders as the direct counter-technique: it offers "no lack of choice when building characters and no trap options, so you still get a functional character even if you pick feats for flavor" — i.e., mathematically auditing every option against a shared power budget so that even a "flavor pick" doesn't create a mechanically dead character — [Lawful Good Rogue / EN World forum synthesis](https://lawfulgoodrogue.com/2022/04/04/pathfinder-2e-and-an-uncharacteristic-review/)
- Related Mike Mearls (5e lead designer) commentary found (not using the exact phrase "trap option," but describing the same underlying design tension): in 5e, "Players had a limited number of choices they could make as they leveled (subclass, feats), and those options were power balanced against a clear benchmark (a feat is worth +2 to an ability score)" — i.e., 5e's technique for avoiding trap options was to keep the *number* of choice points low and benchmark each new option against a fixed reference value — [EN World forum, citing Mearls](https://www.enworld.org/threads/mike-mearls-on-5e-design-he-regrets-bonus-actuons.720616/)

### Inferences
- Two distinct named techniques emerge for avoiding trap options: (1) PF2e's approach — exhaustively audit every option's numeric value against the shared math budget (high designer labor, low trap-option risk, but risk of "bonuses feel small," per the PF2e section above); (2) 5e's approach — deliberately limit the *number* of choice points and benchmark each against one reference unit of power (e.g., "+2 to an ability score") (lower designer labor, but benchmarking against a single flat reference is exactly what produces disparities like feats that are strictly worse than an ASI in most builds). For an "endless customizability" AI-run RPG, this is a direct fork: either (a) invest in an explicit, auditable power-budget formula every new option must satisfy (PF2e-style, more scalable to *procedurally generated* or player-authored options since it's a checkable rule), or (b) rely on a small curated option set benchmarked by a human designer (5e-style, does not scale to "endless" customization without re-introducing trap options as the option list grows).

### Gaps
- No single, definitively "first," citable coinage of the term "trap option" was located (it appears to be organic TTRPG-community jargon rather than a single designer's coined term); this should be presented as community discourse terminology, not an attributed quote.

## Designer blogs/essays on the build-diversity vs. balance tension, and what principles they land on

### Takeaway
Justin Alexander's "The Alexandrian" has a directly relevant, citable trio of essays: "The Many Types of Balance" (taxonomizes balance into Concept/Naturalistic/Spotlight balance, each with distinct tradeoffs), "Fetishizing Balance" (argues against treating balance mechanisms like CR as inviolable law, in favor of flexible GM adjustment), and "Thought of the Day – Optimization Arms Race" (argues optimization is structurally inevitable in any system with meaningful choices, and that GMs should stop escalating against it and instead force diversification or shift focus to narrative consequences).

### Cited Findings
- **Concept Balance**: the principle that "all character concepts should be equally viable" — e.g. a player who wants a melee specialist and one who wants a spellcaster "should both be equally effective in combat"; achieving this "requires limiting either character creation flexibility or gameplay scope" — [The Alexandrian, "The Many Types of Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2500/roleplaying-games/the-many-types-of-balance)
- **Naturalistic Balance**: balance that accepts realistic power disparities given setting/context — e.g. "the martial arts specialist is just not going to be as combat effective as the guy with a machine gun" in a WWII game; this creates "traps" that require system mastery to avoid and can produce "characters who aren't fun to play" — [The Alexandrian, "The Many Types of Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2500/roleplaying-games/the-many-types-of-balance)
- **Spotlight Balance**: balance achieved by the GM actively ensuring "there are equal opportunities for both melee specialists (Conan) and ranged specialists (Robin Hood) to show off their best stuff" — explicitly framed as "more an art than a science," i.e., a GM-runtime technique rather than a character-build-time technique — [The Alexandrian, "The Many Types of Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2500/roleplaying-games/the-many-types-of-balance)
- Alexander's meta-conclusion: there is "no One True Way" among these balance types — different groups will prefer different tradeoffs between character freedom and mechanical certainty — [The Alexandrian, "The Many Types of Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2500/roleplaying-games/the-many-types-of-balance)
- In "Fetishizing Balance," Alexander criticizes both extremes in the 3rd-edition CR debate — "critics who dismiss CR entirely because it isn't foolproof" and "zealots who treat CR as gospel" — arguing "if you fetishize the pursuit of balance in a way that needlessly limits your flexibility, then you're throwing the baby out with the bathwater" — [The Alexandrian, "Fetishizing Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2055/roleplaying-games/fetishizing-balance)
- His "Iron Man Principle": "As long as: (A) There are magic items which are useful (particularly in combat); and (B) The PCs can have those items; and (C) The designers care about game balance at all; then (D) There will need to be guidelines for how many items the PCs should have" — presented as a structural inevitability argument (any system with build-affecting resources needs *some* explicit budget guideline) — [The Alexandrian, "Fetishizing Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2055/roleplaying-games/fetishizing-balance)
- His proposed alternative to rigid balance rules: GMs should simply adjust encounter difficulty to actual observed party power (easier foes for underpowered groups, harder for overpowered ones) rather than treating the system's balance guidelines as immutable law — [The Alexandrian, "Fetishizing Balance"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2055/roleplaying-games/fetishizing-balance)
- In "Optimization Arms Race": "Any system featuring 2+ choices in order to create or advance a character in which those choices have a mechanical impact is going to have people using those choices to optimize their characters" — presented as an inevitability, not a design failure — [The Alexandrian, "Optimization Arms Race"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/35015/roleplaying-games/thought-of-the-day-optimization-arms-race)
- The "arms race" specifically emerges when a GM responds to an optimized character by escalating encounter difficulty, which drives further optimization — Alexander notes optimization "often stems from logical in-character motivation rather than metagaming malice," i.e., it's not inherently a bad-faith act to design against — [The Alexandrian, "Optimization Arms Race"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/35015/roleplaying-games/thought-of-the-day-optimization-arms-race)
- Proposed remedies: (1) **Diversification** — if characters must succeed at *multiple* distinct tasks (not just combat), they structurally cannot hyper-specialize in one area; (2) **Consequence-focused GMing** — "spend less time trying to one-up your players and more time thinking about the interesting stories that result from the consequences of success"; his core prescription is to "opt out of the arms race entirely" rather than design a system that can "win" against optimizers — [The Alexandrian, "Optimization Arms Race"](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/35015/roleplaying-games/thought-of-the-day-optimization-arms-race)

### Inferences
- The Alexandrian's three-part balance taxonomy is directly transferable to an AI-run RPG's design vocabulary: an AI GM has essentially no native ability to do "Spotlight Balance" (it's explicitly described as GM art, not codified mechanics) unless that responsibility is deliberately engineered into the AI's pacing/scene-framing logic — this is a concrete, citable gap the AI-RPG designer should address head-on (i.e., don't assume mechanical/Concept balance is sufficient; Spotlight balance needs its own explicit AI-facing rule or heuristic).
- "Diversification" (forcing multi-domain competence) is a generalizable anti-optimization technique that doesn't require perfect numeric balance — it only requires the *content* (adventures/challenges) to routinely demand skills outside a hyper-optimized build's specialty. This is a lever available to an AI GM at the scenario-generation layer, independent of character-build rules.
- Alexander's Iron Man Principle generalizes beyond magic items: any resource category that (a) meaningfully affects a build's power and (b) is deliberately made available to players will require an explicit acquisition/budget guideline, or balance silently erodes. For "endless customizability," this implies every new player-facing resource axis (crafted items, companions, boons, etc.) needs its own explicit budget rule from day one, not an afterthought.

## Is there existing design discourse about TTRPG systems built to be run by a computer/AI GM rather than a human, and what balance/adjudication concerns come up?

### Takeaway
There is an active but mostly practitioner/product-blog-level (not academic) discourse on AI Game Masters for TTRPGs, converging on a specific concern directly relevant to this project: LLM-driven GMs tend to prioritize narrative flair over rules correctness, creating a documented tension between "story-first" and "rules-first" AI GM architectures, and multiple sources explicitly recommend keeping a human "in the loop" or constraining the AI with well-defined rule boundaries rather than trusting full autonomous adjudication.

### Cited Findings
- Documented risk: "Responses from language models may be skewed, inconsistent, or unexpected, particularly in situations with high stakes or moral complexity, and striking a balance between narrative flow and rule correctness is difficult, as AI may ignore or misunderstand mechanics in favor of theatrical flair" — [search synthesis, aggregating Medium/AI-GM product-blog sources]
- Two divergent product architectures were identified in the current AI-GM tool landscape: (1) **Full rules automation** — systems that provide "full 5e adjudication with combat, conditions, saving throws, and spell slots tracked automatically and applied correctly," and (2) **flexible/general system support** — "AI can interpret rules, manage encounters, and adjudicate edge cases in real time by having users describe their system's mechanics" — [search synthesis, TableForge/aiga.io-type sources]
- A third explicit architecture choice: "story-first vs. rules-first" — "some platforms intentionally use narrative game engines rather than strict rules simulators, capable of running multiple genres while prioritizing living story continuity rather than reproducing every tabletop rule exactly" — [search synthesis]
- Design recommendation converging across sources: "Human supervision and well-defined boundaries are crucial, and AI's optimal usage is when it is directed by careful design and meant to complement human creativity rather than replace it," while noting that "with the right setup, an AI can serve as a genuine game master that maintains tone, enforces rules, tracks NPCs, and generates narrative complications" — [search synthesis, dungeonsdeep.ai / featheredfiction-type sources]
- RPG Research (an established, longer-running TTRPG research organization) maintains a dedicated page/program specifically on "Artificial Intelligence Game Master" — confirming this as an area of sustained, organized interest beyond one-off blog posts, though the specific balance-adjudication findings of that program were not extracted in this pass — [RPG Research](https://rpgresearch.com/ai-gm)

### Inferences
- The "story-first vs. rules-first" fork mirrors the 5e-vs-PF2e philosophical split found elsewhere in this research (improvisational/rulings-based vs. math-first/rules-based). This suggests the AI-GM design space doesn't need a novel axis of thinking — it can directly borrow PF2e's "math-first" philosophy (exhaustive, auditable degree-of-success math; tightly specified action economy) as the safer foundation for autonomous adjudication, since ambiguity-tolerant "rulings over rules" (5e-style) requires exactly the kind of contextual judgment call that LLMs are documented to handle inconsistently under high-stakes/complex conditions.
- No source in this pass distinguished cleanly between "AI GM narrates/improvises" (relatively solved, many products do this) versus "AI GM adjudicates novel/emergent player-invented build combinations for balance" (the harder problem central to this project's brief) — this is the sharpest gap and the one most worth a dedicated follow-up search pass.

All sources found were product marketing/how-to-build blog posts rather than critical design-discourse essays; this is a meaningful gap given the project's specific interest in *failure modes*.
