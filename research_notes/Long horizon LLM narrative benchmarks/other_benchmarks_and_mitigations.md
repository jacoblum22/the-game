# Long-Context/Long-Horizon Consistency Benchmarks and Mitigations (Beyond NCP-Bench and RPGBench)

Note: NCP-Bench (arXiv:2608.08160, "Can LLM Agents Stick to the Script? A Benchmark for Long-Horizon Consistency in Interactive Narratives") and RPGBench are being covered in depth by parallel researchers and are only cross-referenced here for calibration, not deep-dived. One important clarification: **arXiv:2608.08160 is itself the NCP-Bench paper** — it introduces "NCP-Bench (Narrative Commitment Preservation Benchmark)," 100 movie-synopsis-derived narrative environments with fact/commitment ledgers checked via an LLM audit pipeline against an adversarial player agent. Headline numbers (for cross-reference only): best model (GPT-5.2) achieves only a 42% "survival rate" after 20 turns, and fact-conflict rates range 40–68% across six tested LLM narrators. [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)

## Is "Oolong" relevant to interactive-narrative/game-state consistency?

### Takeaway
Oolong (arXiv:2511.02817) is a **poor fit** for the interactive-narrative/game-state consistency question. It is a long-context *aggregation* benchmark built on document/conversation QA-style tasks (classification, counting, distributional reasoning over chunks), not a multi-turn narrative-generation or game-state-tracking benchmark. It is useful only as generic evidence that "long context window" ≠ "genuine use of that context," which is background motivation for the narrative-consistency problem but does not itself measure narrative fact contradiction.

### Cited Findings
- Oolong evaluates whether models "genuinely leverage their full context window," requiring them to "analyz[e] individual chunks of text on an atomic level, and then aggregat[e] these analyses to answer distributional questions" — explicitly contrasted with retrieval-style long-context evals where most tokens can be ignored as noise. — [arXiv:2511.02817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02817)
- It has two splits: Oolong-synth (synthetic, ablatable) and Oolong-real (real-world conversational data), with tasks like classification/counting/temporal-and-user-relationship reasoning — not narrative or game-state tracking. — [arXiv:2511.02817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02817)
- Headline result: "GPT-5, Claude-Sonnet-4, and Gemini-2.5-Pro all achiev[ed] less than 50% accuracy on both splits at 128K" context length. — [arXiv:2511.02817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02817)
- Code/data: [GitHub - abertsch72/oolong](https://github.com/abertsch72/oolong)

### Inferences
- Oolong's finding (frontier models fail at aggregation even well within advertised context limits) is suggestive background support for why narrator agents might also silently lose track of earlier-established facts — the underlying failure mode (context tokens present but not actually integrated/reasoned over) is plausibly related — but Oolong provides no direct evidence about narrative fact contradiction, character memory, or world-state tracking specifically.
- A relevant framing paper, "The Horizon Gap" survey (arXiv:2608.06663), explicitly separates three concepts that are often conflated: **long-context** (model property: token capacity), **long-horizon** (task property: required steps), and **long-term memory** (system property: persistence across steps/sessions). Oolong measures the first; the narrative-consistency question this project cares about is squarely the second and third. — [arXiv:2608.06663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06663)

### Gaps
- No paper found that directly extends Oolong-style aggregation tasks to interactive-fiction/game-master settings.

## What exactly is the "Lost in Stories" taxonomy, and which parts matter for long-session fact forgetting?

### Takeaway
"Lost in Stories: Consistency Bugs in Long Story Generation by LLMs" (arXiv:2603.05890, published March 2026) introduces ConStory-Bench (2,000 prompts, 4 task scenarios) and a taxonomy of 5 categories / 19 subtypes of narrative consistency errors, plus an automated detector (ConStory-Checker). For "forgetting or contradicting established world facts over a long session" specifically, the most relevant categories are **Characterization** (memory/knowledge contradictions) and **World-building & Setting** (rule violations), though the paper's own data shows **Factual & Detail Consistency** and **Timeline & Plot Logic** are the dominant failure modes empirically.

### Cited Findings
- The 5 top-level categories and their 19 subtypes (as extracted from the paper):
  1. **Timeline & Plot Logic** (6 subtypes): Absolute Time Contradictions, Duration Contradictions, Simultaneity Contradictions, Causeless Effects, Causal Logic Violations, Abandoned Plot Elements
  2. **Characterization** (4 subtypes): Memory Contradictions, Knowledge Contradictions, Skill Fluctuations, Forgotten Abilities
  3. **World-building & Setting** (3 subtypes): Core Rules Violations, Social Norms Violations, Geographical Contradictions
  4. **Factual & Detail Consistency** (3 subtypes): Appearance Mismatches, Nomenclature Confusions, Quantitative Mismatches
  5. **Narrative & Style** (3 subtypes): Perspective Confusions, Tone Inconsistencies, Style Shifts
  — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- Directly on-point subtypes for "the AI forgets/contradicts what was already established": **Memory Contradictions** and **Knowledge Contradictions** (character acts as if they don't know something they were told, or knows something they shouldn't) and **Forgotten Abilities** (a character loses/regains established skills inconsistently) under Characterization; **Core Rules Violations** under World-building (the narrator breaks a world rule it itself established). — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- Quantified error rates (Consistency Error Density, CED = errors per 10K words): GPT-5-Reasoning best at 0.113 CED; Claude-Sonnet-4.5 at 0.520 CED; Qwen3-32B at 0.537 CED; open models tested include DeepSeek-V3.2. — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- "Factual & Detail Consistency and Timeline & Plot Logic were 'dominant failure modes'" empirically, despite Characterization/World-building being conceptually closest to "forgetting established facts." — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- Error-location findings: established facts tend to be planted early-to-mid in a narrative (15–30% normalized position), while contradictions of those facts cluster later (40–60% normalized position); errors accumulate roughly **linearly with output length**. Average "gap" (distance between a fact and its later contradiction) varies sharply by error type — geographical contradictions have the largest average gap (31.0% of story length), while perspective confusions have the smallest (4.7%), implying some error types are near-term slips while others are genuine long-range forgetting. — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- Token-level entropy as an early-warning signal: text segments containing consistency errors show measurably higher token-level entropy than clean baseline text (e.g., Qwen3-4B showed a 19.24% entropy increase in erroneous spans), suggesting model "uncertainty" spikes could be used as a live detector for when the narrator is about to contradict itself. — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)
- The paper's own scope, per the fetched content, is detection/characterization of errors, not testing of mitigations — it does **not** report experiments with summarization, RAG, or memory augmentation as fixes. — [arXiv:2603.05890 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)

### Inferences
- Because the average "gap" between a planted fact and its contradiction differs so much by category (4.7%–31.0% of story length), a single flat context window or summarization cadence is unlikely to catch all error types equally — long-gap categories (geography, world rules) need genuinely long-range fact tracking (i.e., an external persistent store), while short-gap categories (perspective/tone) are more like local coherence bugs that ordinary short-context attention should already catch.
- The entropy early-warning signal (a purely internal, cheap-to-compute proxy) is a candidate lightweight mitigation: flagging high-entropy spans for a targeted consistency re-check rather than checking every turn against the full fact store.

### Gaps
- The paper's full experimental section (exact per-category breakdown of CED, and whether ConStory-Checker's contradiction detection was validated against human judgment with a reported precision/recall) was not accessible beyond the taxonomy/summary-level content returned by the fetch tool; only the taxonomy names, top-line CED numbers, and position/entropy statistics above were recoverable from the fetched page.

## Other 2025–2026 benchmarks/papers on long-horizon world-state consistency, narrative memory, and "does it remember N turns ago"

### Takeaway
Beyond NCP-Bench/RPGBench, the relevant new work splits into (a) narrative-specific benchmarks (StoryBench, DREAM/TCM, BOOKMARKS, the detective-game structured-knowledge-tree paper) and (b) general long-term-memory-for-agents benchmarks not narrative-specific but methodologically on point (LOCOMO and its many follow-ons, MERIT/FAMA-style cost-aware evaluations, Memora/"From Recall to Forgetting"). The general-agent-memory literature is where most of the *quantified* mitigation evidence currently lives; narrative-specific papers are newer and thinner on numbers.

### Cited Findings
**Narrative/role-play-specific:**
- **StoryBench** (arXiv:2506.13356) is "a dynamic benchmark for evaluating long-term memory" built from an interactive-fiction domain with "dynamically branching storylines with complex reasoning structures," including a mode requiring the model to "independently trace back and revise earlier choices after failure." Its current interactive-fiction dataset is small (6 chapters per one aggregator description). No model-level accuracy numbers were retrievable from the fetched abstract content. — [arXiv:2506.13356](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13356)
- **DREAM** (arXiv:2608.05170, accepted KDD 2026) proposes an Event-aware Memory Graph (EMG) — inspired by the Activating-Event-Belief-Consequence cognitive model — that converts unstructured literary text into temporally ordered, causally linked events, supporting dual-granularity character profiles (stable traits + event-driven evolution). It introduces the **Temporal Causal Memory (TCM) benchmark** specifically to measure role-play agents' temporal consistency, memory accuracy, and causal narrative ability, and reports state-of-the-art results (without task-specific fine-tuning) on TCM plus two existing role-play-agent benchmarks. — [arXiv:2608.05170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05170)
- **BOOKMARKS** (arXiv:2605.14169) is an "active storyline memory" method for role-play: instead of compressing history into summaries (which "discards details which become inaccessible to subsequent grounding"), it keeps the full preceding storyline accessible and retrieves task-relevant content on demand, using "passive updating" checkpoints ("bookmarks") to avoid re-processing the whole history on every turn. Evaluated on 47 characters across 6 narratives, 9,537 test cases: **mystery-game reasoning accuracy improved from 39.44% to 45.42% over the strongest baseline**, plus fidelity gains across emotion/intent/causality/position/content dimensions of next-action prediction, with lower compute overhead than full re-summarization. — [arXiv:2605.14169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14169)
- **Structured Knowledge Trees for LLM detective games** (arXiv:2609.23043) targets narrative reliability and "epistemic pacing" (controlling when information is allowed to be revealed) using a tri-agent pipeline (knowledge retrieval / dialogue generation / response verification) constrained by a knowledge tree that encodes what a virtual suspect is permitted to reveal given the current narrative state. Evaluated on a playable game ("The Interrogation of Adrian Gale") with a formal user study: **reduces critical hallucinations by 64.78%** and "entirely prevents premature narrative disclosure," at the cost of usability trade-offs from "forced conversational reveals." — [arXiv:2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23043)

**General long-term-memory-for-agents (not narrative-specific, but methodologically transferable):**
- **LOCOMO** is a widely-used long-term conversational memory benchmark: 10 multi-session conversations, ~600 turns / ~16K tokens each, 1,986 QA pairs across Single-Hop/Multi-Hop/Temporal/Open-Domain reasoning categories. — [EmergentMind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/locomo-benchmark-d06cff1a-d4a5-4df8-ab85-fdca157d190b)
- Reported LOCOMO accuracy varies a lot by memory architecture: RGMem reports 86.17% overall, beating the strongest baseline Zep (79.09%) by over 7 points; a "Mode A Retrieval" approach reports 74.8% aggregate (no cloud dependency) vs. Mem0's 64.2%; Synthius-Mem reports 94.37% overall (96.73% single-hop, 94.34% multi-hop, **89.32% temporal**, 77.33% open-domain) using GPT-4.1-mini as both answer model and judge. — [search aggregation, arXiv:2604.11563 (Synthius-Mem), arXiv:2510.16392 (RGMem)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11563)
- Note the pattern across all of these: **temporal reasoning and open-domain reasoning are consistently the weakest categories** relative to single-hop fact lookup — directly analogous to the narrative-consistency problem of tracking "what changed and when" versus simple fact retrieval.
- **"When Does Memory Help? A Cost-Aware Evaluation of Long-Term Memory in Tool-Using LLM Agents"** (arXiv:2609.05441) introduces MERIT (marginal utility of memory under explicit cost accounting) and FAMA (Forgetting-Aware Memory Accuracy, which penalizes reliance on obsolete/invalidated memory). Key quantified findings: on **updated facts**, embedding retrieval accuracy "collapses unpredictably" (0.30–0.95 range across models, with a 0.45 max-seed gap — i.e., highly unstable), while update-on-write structured stores and LLM summarization both stayed in a much tighter 0.70–1.00 band. Even when the correct (updated) fact was retrieved, agents acted on it only **55% of the time**. Memory raised dependent-task success from a verified floor of 0.00 up to 0.55–1.00 depending on model (GPT-4.1, Claude Haiku 4.5) and implementation, and "swapping a memory's implementation moves task success by up to 60 points" — i.e., architecture choice matters as much as whether you have memory at all. Optimal configurations delivered 2.7–3.9x the marginal utility per dollar of the worst configuration. — [arXiv:2609.05441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)
- **"From Recall to Forgetting: Benchmarking Long-Term Memory for Personalized Agents"** (arXiv:2604.20006) introduces the Memora benchmark (week-to-month-long conversations) and the same FAMA-style metric idea, testing 4 LLMs and 6 memory agents. Its central qualitative finding: agents show "frequent reuse of invalid memories and failures to reconcile evolving memories," and across the board "memory agents offer marginal improvements" over baselines with no memory at all — i.e., today's bolt-on memory systems are not a slam-dunk fix. Specific accuracy/degradation numbers were not recoverable from the fetched abstract content. — [arXiv:2604.20006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20006)
- **"Governing Evolving Memory in LLM Agents" / SSGM framework** (arXiv:2603.11768) explicitly names "semantic drift" — knowledge degrading through iterative summarization — as a memory corruption risk, and proposes consistency verification, temporal decay modeling, and dynamic access control gating before any memory consolidation step. The fetched content did not include quantified before/after numbers for SSGM's effectiveness (full paper needed). — [arXiv:2603.11768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11768)
- The "Horizon Gap" survey (arXiv:2608.06663) reviewed 1,547 arXiv papers (2024–2026) and frames the cross-cutting pattern: as task horizons lengthen, "outcome-only signals grow uninformative," pushing the field toward denser step-level feedback (process reward models, trajectory diagnostics) rather than end-of-task grading alone — relevant to why narrative-consistency benchmarks (NCP-Bench, ConStory-Bench) are moving toward per-turn/per-claim auditing rather than end-of-story judgment. — [arXiv:2608.06663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06663)
- Related survey/roadmap resource: [GitHub - RUC-NLPIR/Awesome-Long-Horizon-Agents](https://github.com/RUC-NLPIR/Awesome-Long-Horizon-Agents)

### Inferences
- Across both narrative-specific and general-agent-memory papers, the single most consistent quantified pattern is: **plain embedding/vector retrieval is the least reliable option specifically for facts that change over time** (LOCOMO's weak "temporal" category across systems; the 0.30–0.95 unstable range for embedding retrieval on updated facts in arXiv:2609.05441). Structured, explicitly-updated stores ("update-on-write") and full-storyline-with-targeted-retrieval approaches (BOOKMARKS) consistently outperform naive summarize-and-forget or pure semantic-similarity retrieval on exactly the "did something change since we last talked about it" axis that matters most for narrative/game-state consistency.
- Retrieval accuracy alone is not sufficient: the finding that agents act on a correctly-retrieved fact only 55% of the time (arXiv:2609.05441) suggests that even a perfect external memory store does not fully solve consistency — the generation step itself needs to actually use retrieved facts, which argues for something like the detective-game paper's verification agent (arXiv:2609.23043) that checks generated output against the knowledge store post-hoc, not just retrieval that feeds the prompt.
- "Memory agents offer marginal improvements" (arXiv:2604.20006) is an important counterweight to the more impressive-sounding gains reported by individual system papers (e.g., BOOKMARKS' 39.44%→45.42%, RGMem's 86.17% vs Zep's 79.09%): those gains are typically measured against a specific set of baselines on a specific benchmark, and the broader benchmarking literature suggests real-world gains from bolting memory onto an agent are often modest and inconsistent across tasks.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that runs the *same* mitigation techniques (RAG, structured extraction, periodic summarization, self-check) head-to-head specifically on a narrative/game-master consistency benchmark (as opposed to general conversational-memory QA benchmarks like LOCOMO or personalized-agent benchmarks like Memora). The narrative-specific papers found (DREAM/TCM, BOOKMARKS, structured knowledge trees) each propose and validate one architecture against generic baselines rather than systematically comparing summarization vs. RAG vs. structured-store vs. self-check under matched conditions in a narrative setting.
- Full quantified results for SSGM (arXiv:2603.11768) and StoryBench (arXiv:2506.13356) were not accessible via abstract-level fetch; a full-text PDF read would be needed for exact numbers.

## Mitigation strategies: quantified evidence of what works, and where summarization itself breaks down

### Takeaway
The clearest, most directly quantified findings are: (1) naive iterative LLM summarization measurably discards low-frequency/atypical details ("summarization drift") and is beaten by leave-full-text-and-retrieve approaches (BOOKMARKS) and by plain observation-masking in agentic-task settings; (2) structured/update-on-write external stores are far more stable than embedding retrieval specifically for facts that change over time; (3) a verification/audit step layered on top of generation (checking output against a structured fact/knowledge store) gives the largest single reported consistency win found in this search (-64.78% critical hallucinations in the detective-game paper). No paper gives a precise numeric "threshold" (e.g., "after N summarization passes, X% of facts are lost"), but there is qualitative consensus on what causes the failure and what to preserve verbatim vs. compress.

### Cited Findings
- **Summarization drift is a named, general phenomenon**: "each compression pass silently discards low-frequency details, and after enough passes, the agent 'remembers' a sanitized, generic version of history that fails on edge cases." — [Morph: Compaction vs Summarization](https://www.morphllm.com/compaction-vs-summarization)
- Verbatim compaction (as opposed to LLM-generated summarization) has a lower compression ratio (50–70%) but "what you keep is guaranteed accurate," whereas summarization achieves higher compression at the cost of information loss. — [Morph: Compaction vs Summarization](https://www.morphllm.com/compaction-vs-summarization)
- Recommended verbatim-vs-compress split found in the practitioner literature: **preserve verbatim** — precise facts, code/numerical results, direct quotes, and the most recent ~10% of context (active working memory: current tool call, most recent message, immediate prior reasoning); **safe to compress/summarize** — narrative/scene-setting context, reasoning chains already acted on, and redundant observations. — [Morph: Compaction vs Summarization](https://www.morphllm.com/compaction-vs-summarization)
- Practical rule of thumb reported: use verbatim compaction for the *first* compression cycle; if a second compaction is needed, prefer handing off to a fresh agent/context rather than re-summarizing an already-summarized history (i.e., avoid compounding summarization passes). — [Morph: Compaction vs Summarization](https://www.morphllm.com/compaction-vs-summarization)
- In agentic (non-narrative) task settings, "observation masking outperform[ed] LLM summarization in terms of overall efficiency and reliability — in four out of five test settings, agents using observation masking paid less per problem and often performed better," suggesting that for some tasks, simply hiding/truncating old tool outputs beats generating a summary of them. — [search aggregation, cited from agent-memory literature](https://medium.com/the-ai-forum/automatic-context-compression-in-llm-agents-why-agents-need-to-forget-and-how-to-help-them-do-it-43bff14c341d)
- BOOKMARKS' explicit critique of incremental summarization (used as its baseline comparator, "profiling"): incremental summarization "discards details which become inaccessible to subsequent grounding" — and BOOKMARKS' full-storyline-plus-targeted-retrieval alternative improved mystery-game reasoning accuracy from 39.44% to 45.42% over that summarization-based baseline. — [arXiv:2605.14169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14169)
- Structured knowledge tree + verification-agent mitigation (closest thing found to a "forced periodic re-verification/self-check step" for narrative consistency): **64.78% reduction in critical hallucinations** and complete prevention of premature information disclosure, at the cost of some conversational naturalness/usability. — [arXiv:2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23043)
- On RAG/structured-store stability specifically for facts that change: update-on-write and LLM-summarization memory stayed within 0.70–1.00 accuracy on updated facts, vs. embedding retrieval's unstable 0.30–0.95 range (0.45 max-seed gap) — the clearest quantified evidence that architecture choice for "did this fact change" matters enormously, and that plain vector-similarity RAG is the fragile option for this specific problem. — [arXiv:2609.05441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)
- Even with a working memory/retrieval system, the generation step frequently fails to act on the correct fact (55% utilization rate on correctly-retrieved facts, arXiv:2609.05441) — implying that retrieval/summarization-side fixes have a ceiling unless paired with a generation-time check.

### Inferences
- The evidence converges on a layered strategy for a long-running interactive narrator: (1) maintain an external, structurally-updated (not purely embedding-retrieved) fact store for anything that can change state (character knowledge, relationships, item ownership, established world rules); (2) avoid compounding/iterative LLM summarization of the same material more than once — prefer verbatim retention of recent turns plus a single compression pass, or full-text-with-targeted-retrieval (BOOKMARKS-style) over repeated summarize-of-a-summary; (3) add a post-hoc verification/audit step that checks generated narrative content against the fact store before it's shown to the user, since retrieval alone does not guarantee the generator will use the fact correctly (55% utilization finding) — this is exactly the largest quantified win found in this search (64.78% hallucination reduction).
- No source in this search quantifies "at what point" (which summarization pass, what compression ratio, what turn count) drift crosses from tolerable to harmful — this remains a genuine open gap in the literature as of the papers found.

### Gaps
- No paper found gives a precise numeric threshold for when periodic summarization starts to lose/corrupt facts (e.g., "after N passes" or "past M% compression ratio, error rate rises by X%"). The "summarization drift" concept is well-attested qualitatively (Morph, SSGM's framing, BOOKMARKS' critique of "profiling") but not pinned to a number in any source found here.
- No head-to-head experiment was found comparing summarization vs. RAG vs. structured-store vs. self-check under identical conditions specifically on a narrative-consistency task (the closest is arXiv:2609.05441's MERIT framework, which is for tool-using agents generally, not narrative generation).
- The full-text results of SSGM (arXiv:2603.11768) — which most directly targets "semantic drift from iterative summarization" as a named problem with a proposed governance fix — were not retrievable beyond the abstract in this search; a full-PDF read would likely contain the missing quantified before/after data.
