# TTRPG Degrees of Success and Action Economy Resolution Mechanics

## DEGREES OF SUCCESS RESOLUTION SYSTEMS

### How does Pathfinder 2e's four-degree system work exactly?

#### Takeaway
Pathfinder 2e uses a four-degree system where success or failure is determined by comparing the roll result to the DC by margins of 10 points, with natural 20s/1s shifting one degree better/worse regardless of the actual numerical margin.

#### Cited Findings
- The four degrees are: Critical Success (beat DC by 10+), Success (beat DC by 1-9), Failure (miss DC by 1-9), and Critical Failure (miss DC by 10+) — [Step 4: Degree of Success - Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2286)
- If you roll a natural 20 on the die, your result is one degree of success better than it would be by numbers alone; a natural 1 makes your result one degree worse — [Step 4: Degree of Success - Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2286)
- Critical successes and failures only occur if you exceed or miss the DC by 10 or more (unless a natural 20/1 shifts you into that degree) — [Pathfinder (2e): Criticals and Degrees of Success - Rule's Gaming Website](https://rule.lol/guides/pathfinder-2e-criticals-and-degrees-of-success/)
- Some actions and abilities have stronger effects on a critical success or failure—for example, a Strike deals double damage on a critical hit — [Step 4: Determine the Degree of Success and Effect - Pathfinder 2e](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2286&Redirected=1)
- The system creates asymmetry where natural 20s and 1s always shift the result, even if you would have succeeded/failed without the roll adjustment — [Step 4: Degree of Success - Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2286)

#### Inferences
- The 10-point margin system creates threshold-based gradations where DCs are designed with these boundaries in mind, making PC and NPC ability modifiers directly affect which degree tier they reach
- The natural 20/1 rule ensures no roll is trivial—even a high modifier might only achieve success against a DC, and even a low modifier might crit-fail
- By explicitly calling out critical effects, designers can create action economy advantages (like double damage) that reward lucky/skillful rolls without changing base mechanics

#### Gaps
- None significant for this question; the mechanic is well-documented in official sources

### How do OTHER systems implement graduated/multi-tier success?

#### Takeaway
Multi-tier systems vary widely: FATE uses a four-outcome ladder (fail/tie/succeed/succeed-with-style), Blades in the Dark separates position (risky-ness) from effect (impact) as independent axes, Genesys/Star Wars tracks successes and advantages as separate, counterable currencies, and World of Darkness counts individual successes from dice pools to create a sliding scale.

#### Cited Findings

**FATE Core System:**
- FATE has four core outcomes: Failure (roll lower than opposition), Tie (roll equal), Succeed (win by 1-2 shifts on the ladder), and Succeed with Style (win by more) — [Four Outcomes • Fate Core](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes)
- FATE uses a numerical ladder (+8 Legendary through -2 Terrible) with narrative values; each level on the ladder above the opposition is one "shift" — [Taking Action, Dice, & The Ladder • Fate Core](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/taking-action-dice-the-ladder)
- On a tie, the player typically gets what they want but at a minor cost, or gets a lesser version of their intended result — [Four Outcomes • Fate Core](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes)

**Blades in the Dark Position-and-Effect:**
- Position (controlled/risky/desperate) and Effect (limited/standard/great) are set by the GM independently before rolling — [Setting Position & Effect | Blades in the Dark RPG](https://bladesinthedark.com/setting-position-effect)
- Position is "how bad can things go?" and Effect is "how much will this action achieve?" — [Blades in the Dark – System Cheat Sheet (v3)](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/41025/roleplaying-games/blades-in-the-dark-system-cheat-sheet-v3)
- This creates nine possible combinations (3x3 grid), allowing the GM to convey different fictional circumstances before the player rolls — [Setting Position & Effect | Blades in the Dark RPG](https://bladesinthedark.com/setting-position-effect)
- The resolution mechanic (critical/success/partial/failure) interacts with these preset position/effect conditions to determine narrative outcomes — [Blades in the Dark – System Cheat Sheet](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/40484/roleplaying-games/blades-in-the-dark-system-cheat-sheet)

**Genesys/Star Wars Narrative Dice:**
- Results include Success/Failure symbols and Advantage/Threat symbols as completely independent axes — [Let's Study Genesys] Part 1: Narrative Dice & Basic Rules](https://philgamer.wordpress.com/2018/07/25/lets-study-genesys-part-1-narrative-dice-basic-rules/)
- Genesys allows for success with a setback (failure with advantage, creating narrative tension) or failure with upside — [Dice Full Of Drama: The Narrative Dice System](https://www.mattgoesrogue.com/genesys-narrative-dice/)
- Player dice successes/advantages cancel out corresponding opposition failures/threats, leaving the net result uncanceled — [Genesys In-Depth | Cannibal Halfling Gaming](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2020/09/16/genesys-in-depth/)
- The dice system creates situations where you can succeed but suffer complications, or fail but gain a benefit — [Let's Study Genesys] Part 1: Narrative Dice & Basic Rules](https://philgamer.wordpress.com/2018/07/25/lets-study-genesys-part-1-narrative-dice-basic-rules/)

**World of Darkness Dice Pools:**
- Characters roll a number of 10-sided dice equal to attribute plus skill; each die showing 6+ counts as one success — [World of Darkness Dice Pools - William Pees](https://enantiodromos.substack.com/p/world-of-darkness-dice-pools)
- Higher counts of successes reflect a greater degree of accomplishment across a sliding scale — [Four Dice Pool Systems All Gamers Should Know – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/four-die-pool-systems-all-gamers-should-know/)
- Classic World of Darkness used difficulty target numbers (default 6+) that could be shifted up or down based on challenge level — [World of Darkness Dice Pools - William Pees](https://enantiodromos.substack.com/p/world-of-darkness-dice-pools)
- New World of Darkness simplified this to target 8+, removing the "ones reduce successes" mechanic but limiting maximum successes — [World Of Darkness Rules – EpicSavingThrow.com](https://epicsavingthrow.com/world-of-darkness-rules/)

#### Inferences
- Blades in the Dark's separation of position and effect is unique in allowing GMs to frame difficulty and impact as independent narrative concerns—a risky but standard-effect action vs. a controlled but limited-effect action tell different stories
- FATE's "tie" outcome is designed specifically to keep play moving even on an even roll, contrasting with systems that treat ties as complete successes or failures
- Genesys/Star Wars approach treats success/failure and advantage/threat as orthogonal resources, allowing for nuanced outcomes that pure success-degree systems cannot express (success with complications is different from critical failure)
- World of Darkness systems scale naturally with larger dice pools, inherently creating more degrees of success as characters specialize (more dice = more successes possible)
- Binary success/failure systems (like D&D 5e base mechanics) exist in contrast to all these approaches, suggesting designers chose degrees of success specifically for narrative/mechanical richness

#### Gaps
- Limited documentation on why FATE chose exactly four outcomes versus three or five
- No design interviews found comparing these systems' trade-offs directly from designers' perspectives
- Scarce analysis of computational complexity differences between these approaches in actual play

### What are the documented tradeoffs between these approaches?

#### Takeaway
Degree-of-success systems are more mechanically complex but provide richer narrative texture and more frequent "interesting" outcomes; binary systems are computationally simpler but may lead to less engaging partial-success scenarios, while dice-pool approaches scale naturally with character power.

#### Cited Findings

**Complexity vs. Narrative Richness:**
- Degrees of success are more complicated compared to binary success and failure, but create opportunities for "succeed at a cost" mechanics that can be more interesting than just binary outcomes — [TTRPG degrees of success versus binary pass fail mechanics comparison - RPG Museum](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Degree_of_success)
- The most interesting aspects of narrative lie in the "sweet spot of partial success," where characters stumble forward from complication to complication, providing progress and earned victories with stakes that move the narrative — [The cost of partial success - Cezar Capacle talks Game Design](https://capacle.substack.com/p/the-cost-of-partial-success)
- Conflict resolution mechanics don't need binary results; many systems have moved beyond simple binary success/failure by including critical success/critical failure and systems like Star Wars using advantages/threats independent of success — [Degree of success | RPG Museum](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Degree_of_success)

**Dice Pool Scaling:**
- Dice pool systems (like World of Darkness) are particularly effective at achieving multiple tiers of success, as each success can be another tier — [Four Dice Pool Systems All Gamers Should Know – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/four-die-pool-systems-all-gamers-should-know/)
- This approach naturally scales: more powerful characters with more dice inherently have more success degrees available, without requiring additional resolution rules — [World of Darkness Dice Pools - William Pees](https://enantiodromos.substack.com/p/world-of-darkness-dice-pools)

**Narrative Complexity Trade-offs:**
- In narrative-heavy games, binary decision points create exponential narrative branching (10 binary choices = 1,024 paths), making full branching impractical; degree-of-success systems reduce this by having fewer distinct outcomes — [The cost of partial success - Cezar Capacle talks Game Design](https://capacle.substack.com/p/the-cost-of-partial-success)

#### Inferences
- Systems designed for tactical/strategic depth (like Pathfinder 2e) embrace fixed degree counts because they can be precisely balanced; story-heavy systems (like FATE, Blades) use degrees primarily to avoid binary stagnation
- The FATE tie outcome specifically addresses the problem that binary systems have no graceful "neither player achieved dominance" state
- Genesys's independent success/advantage axes solve the problem that a single success-degree number cannot simultaneously express "I did it" and "but something else happened"
- Dice pool systems achieve degrees through quantity (count of successes) rather than thresholds, making them less dependent on finely-tuned DC math

#### Gaps
- No systematic comparison of how often each system lands on each degree of success in typical play (e.g., how often does Pathfinder 2e produce critical successes at RAW vs. modified DCs?)
- Limited data on player perception of narrative quality across these degree-of-success approaches
- Designer intent documents are sparse; most information comes from fan analysis rather than published design manifestos

## ACTION ECONOMY DESIGN

### How do different systems structure what a character can do in a turn?

#### Takeaway
Systems vary from D&D 5e's fixed action/bonus-action/reaction split, to Pathfinder 2e's flexible 3-action pool where all actions cost equally, to 4th edition D&D's Standard/Move/Minor breakdown with action points, to Lancer's mecha-specific structure of move + (two quick actions OR one full action) + free actions and overcharge.

#### Cited Findings

**D&D 5e Action Economy:**
- Each creature gets one action, one bonus action, and one reaction per round — [Actions, Bonus Actions, and Reactions Rules](https://dndrules.com/actions-bonus-actions-reactions)
- An action can be used to attack, cast a spell, dash, disengage, dodge, help, hide, ready, search, or use an object — [D&D 5e quick reference](https://crobi.github.io/dnd5e-quickref/preview/quickref.html)
- Bonus actions only exist if a specific ability, spell, or item grants one; you cannot downsize a regular action into a bonus action, making them conditional — [D&D 5e Action Economy Explained - Tabletop Joab](https://tabletopjoab.com/action-economy-in-dd-5e-explained/)
- A reaction is a response to a trigger occurring on any turn, with one per round refreshed at the start of your next turn (e.g., Shield spell, Counterspell, Opportunity Attack) — [D&D Action Economy Explained (5e) - Roll for Two](https://www.rollfortwo.com/articles/dnd-action-economy-explained/)
- If you cast a spell as a bonus action in a round, you can only cast cantrips with your action for the rest of that turn — [Taking Advantage of Bonus Actions & Reactions in 5e D&D](https://www.hipstersanddragons.com/maximising-bonus-actions-and-reactions/)

**Pathfinder 2e Three-Action Economy:**
- Each round, characters can perform up to three actions plus one reaction — [Action Economy - Rules - Pathfinder 2e - Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=1026)
- Most actions cost 1 action (Strike, Step, Seek, Interact, Raise a Shield); some cost 2 (most spells, many activities); a few cost 3 (some powerful spells) — [A Beginner's Guide to PATHFINDER's 2e Action Economy - Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- Movement consumes the same resource as attacking, making positioning, buff actions, and utility meaningful rather than free — [A Beginner's Guide to PATHFINDER's 2e Action Economy - Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- Each Strike after the first in a turn takes a −5 penalty (−4 with agile weapons), with the third at −10 (−8 agile), discouraging pure multiple-attack spam — [A Beginner's Guide to PATHFINDER's 2e Action Economy - Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- Design philosophy: every action has equal weight, and a two-action activity still leaves room for a little movement or a second basic attack, while a three-action activity soaks up the whole turn — [Pathfinder 2e Action Economy in ICRPG - RUNEHAMMER](https://forums.runehammer.online/t/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-in-icrpg/4396)

**4th Edition D&D Action Economy:**
- Each round provides a Standard Action, a Move Action, and a Minor Action, plus one Opportunity Action (Opportunity Attack) per turn — [D&D 4E - The Economy of Actions in 4e | EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/the-economy-of-actions-in-4e.235001/)
- Players can gain additional actions via Action Points earned by completing encounters without extended rests (milestones) — [D&D 4E - The Economy of Actions in 4e | EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/the-economy-of-actions-in-4e.235001/)
- Powers are divided into at-will (usable every round as standard action), encounter (once per encounter), and daily (once per day) — [Action Economics 101 | Everyman Gaming](https://gamingeveryman.wordpress.com/2014/02/24/55/)
- The system heavily focused on grid-based combat with precise movement ranges and areas of effect — [Action Economics 101 | Everyman Gaming](https://gamingeveryman.wordpress.com/2014/02/24/55/)

**Lancer Mecha TTRPG:**
- Every mech gets a move action plus either two quick actions OR one full action, plus free actions and an overcharge option for extra actions at cost of heat — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)
- Quick actions include Skirmish (attack), Boost (extra movement), Quick Tech (invade/hack), or activate a system — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)
- NPCs cannot Brace (take a defensive stance) as a reaction, meaning they eat full damage unless they have mitigation systems, and NPCs cannot Overcharge, limiting them to two quick actions per turn — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)
- The system intentionally creates asymmetries between PCs and NPCs to encourage tactical play around those differences — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)

#### Inferences
- D&D 5e's rigid action/bonus/reaction split is designed for simple learning and fast play, but creates "bonus action economy" as a secondary metagame for optimization
- Pathfinder 2e's unified action pool with variable costs is explicitly designed to make positioning and utility decisions meaningful in the same way as attacks
- 4th edition's encounter/daily power structure ties action economy to resource management (how many powers you've used this encounter) rather than just turn structure
- Lancer's move + (2 quick OR 1 full) creates a meaningful strategic choice each turn (do I want flexibility or focus?), and NPC asymmetry is a deliberate design pattern to keep encounters solvable
- 4th edition's focus on grid-based, tactical positioning required the rigid Standard/Move/Minor structure to be comprehensible within movement rules

#### Gaps
- Limited documentation of why D&D 5e retained the bonus action as a separate resource rather than folding it into a unified pool
- Sparse analysis of action economy differences in non-combat scenarios (social encounters, exploration) across systems
- No published designer commentary on the trade-offs between Pathfinder 2e and 4th edition's approaches

### What documented exploits or balance problems have arisen from action economy design?

#### Takeaway
The primary documented action economy problems are: D&D 5e's bonus-action optimization ("is my character doing something every turn?"), summoning/ally multiplication that the DMG doesn't adequately address, and Haste stacking effects that grant additional actions, all of which can overwhelm single enemies through action count rather than tactical skill.

#### Cited Findings

**Summoning and Ally Problems:**
- When PCs have summoned monsters or friendly NPCs on their side, there are huge balance issues, but the Dungeon Master's Guide doesn't specify how this factors into encounter balancing math — [Balancing the Battlefield: Mastering Action Economy in D&D 5e Encounters](https://www.boardnbyte.com/post/balancing-the-battlefield-mastering-action-economy-in-d-d-5e-encounters)
- Many potent builds maximize actions through summoned allies or effects like Haste — [Balancing the Battlefield: Mastering Action Economy in D&D 5e Encounters](https://www.boardnbyte.com/post/balancing-the-battlefield-mastering-action-economy-in-d-d-5e-encounters)
- D&D doesn't want players to be "minion masters," with action economy being the primary reason (along with combat speed) — [DnD 5e Action Economy - The Dungeon Rats](https://thedungeonrats.com/dnd-5e-action-economy/)
- The action economy favors the side with more "bodies" on the field, regardless of individual power levels — [Dungeons & Dragons: What Is Action Economy? - TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/dungeons-dragons-what-is-action-economy-explained/)

**Bonus Action Economy Imbalance:**
- Some players optimize their character build to have a Bonus Action to use every round, which makes their turns feel more fulfilling than a player with only one action, even if that single action is more powerful than an action/Bonus Action combo — [Top Strategies for Balancing Combat Encounters - TTRPGs](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/top-strategies-for-balancing-combat-encounters/)
- There's a documented issue where certain classes (particularly Rogues, Monks, Spellcasters with bonus action spells) have more "turn fulfillment" than single-action classes — [Action Economy | DungeonHeaven](https://www.dungeonheaven.com/post/action-economy-in-dungeons-dragons-5e)

**General Action Economy Imbalance Effects:**
- Imbalances can distort battle outcomes, making them overly dependent on the presence of particular units (summoned creatures, allies) rather than on player strategy, which diminishes the game's strategic depth and can frustrate players — [Top Strategies for Balancing Combat Encounters - TTRPGs](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/top-strategies-for-balancing-combat-encounters/)
- The core issue is that one side having more "turns worth of stuff" than another is virtually impossible to overcome through tactics alone — [DnD 5e Action Economy - The Dungeon Rats](https://thedungeonrats.com/dnd-5e-action-economy/)

#### Inferences
- D&D 5e's system allows exploits not through intentional design but through accumulation (summons are fine individually, Haste is fine individually, but together they break balance math)
- Pathfinder 2e avoids these problems by making summoned allies compete for the party's limited action pool (a summoned creature uses the summoner's reaction/action economy)
- The bonus action problem suggests that action quality is not well-distributed in D&D 5e—some bonus actions (Rogues' Cunning Action dash) are universally valuable while others are niche, creating power disparities
- The documented lack of guidance in the DMG for handling summoned creatures suggests this was an oversight rather than a solved design problem

#### Gaps
- No quantitative analysis of how much action economy advantage a typical summoner build achieves (e.g., "adds 1.8x actions per round")
- Limited data on Pathfinder 2e's success in avoiding these problems (are summon-heavy builds equally dominant there?)
- No systematic comparison of Lancer's approach to action economy balance vs. other systems (Lancer explicitly gives NPCs asymmetric action budgets to stay relevant)

### What design techniques do systems use to prevent one build from simply having "more turns worth of stuff" than another?

#### Takeaway
Pathfinder 2e uses fixed action pools and variable action costs to force trade-offs; D&D 5e relies on resource limits (spells per day, limited use abilities) rather than action economy guardrails; 4th edition ties action availability to encounter pacing via daily/encounter powers; Lancer uses asymmetric NPC mechanics and overcharge heat costs as trade-offs.

#### Cited Findings

**Pathfinder 2e's Approach:**
- Pathfinder 2e uses a three-action economy to ensure every class feels impactful during gameplay, preventing any one character from dominating a turn — [RPG Class Balancing | TTRPG Games](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-rpg-class-balancing)
- The system prevents dominant builds by introducing trade-offs (e.g., resource limits or situational abilities) and managing the action economy to avoid any class dominating turns — [RPG Class Balancing | TTRPG Games](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-rpg-class-balancing)
- Variable action costs force meaningful choices: should you use all three actions to cast a spell, or use fewer actions to leave room for mobility/defense? — [A Beginner's Guide to PATHFINDER's 2e Action Economy - Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)
- Diminishing returns on attacks (−5 second attack, −10 third attack) prevent "just attack three times" from always being optimal — [A Beginner's Guide to PATHFINDER's 2e Action Economy - Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)

**D&D 5e's Approach (Resource-Based):**
- D&D 5e relies on daily/encounter resource limits (spell slots, limited-use abilities like Bardic Inspiration) rather than hard action economy caps — [Action Economy in TTRPGs | Game Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CHTYgLsq_q8)
- The system assumes resource scarcity will naturally limit the most powerful builds; if a build can do powerful things every turn, resources run out faster — [Top Strategies for Balancing Combat Encounters - TTRPGs](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/top-strategies-for-balancing-combat-encounters/)

**Design Philosophy Against Build Dominance:**
- Breaking up actions in different ways—giving players an "offensive action," a "defensive action," and a "neutral" action—changes how the game plays and prevents one action type from dominating — [Top Strategies for Balancing Combat Encounters - TTRPGs](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/top-strategies-for-balancing-combat-encounters/)
- A well-designed system gives each class a clear purpose, avoids overpowered abilities, and prevents redundancy — [RPG Class Balancing | TTRPG Games](https://www.ttrpg-games.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-rpg-class-balancing)

**Lancer's Approach (Asymmetric Design):**
- Lancer deliberately creates asymmetries between player characters and NPCs: NPCs cannot Brace or Overcharge, limiting their action economy and making them more vulnerable than PCs would be in the same situation — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)
- Overcharge allows extra actions at a heat cost, creating a mechanical trade-off (more actions now = more vulnerable to heat-based damage later) — [Lancer vs D&D: How Combat Actually Works - Kazkar.ai Blog](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)

#### Inferences
- Pathfinder 2e's approach is most directly addressing action economy balance through hard caps and scaling diminishing returns, treating it as a solved design problem
- D&D 5e's resource-based approach is less about action economy structure and more about attrition—it assumes the most powerful action-economy builds will burn resources faster
- Lancer's asymmetric approach (NPCs can't Brace/Overcharge like PCs) is a meta-design solution: rather than perfectly balancing PCs and NPCs, the system acknowledges they operate under different rules and builds tactical advantage into that difference
- 4th edition's approach of tying actions to the encounter/daily cycle rather than turn-by-turn structure is distinct from all others; it's attempting to control action economy at the fight level rather than the turn level

#### Gaps
- No empirical data on whether Pathfinder 2e actually succeeds at preventing action-economy dominant builds in practice
- Limited documentation of whether D&D 5e's resource-based approach actually prevents bonus-action-heavy builds from dominating turns (it controls power over the whole day, but not individual turn dominance)
- No systematic analysis of how Lancer's NPC asymmetry affects overall encounter balance compared to symmetric systems
- Missing documentation of whether variable action costs (PF2e's 1/2/3 action spells) actually achieve equal "turn fulfillment" or if some cost-to-effect ratios are still obviously superior

## RESEARCH NOTES SUMMARY

**Data Collected:**
- Pathfinder 2e: 4-degree system, three-action economy
- Blades in the Dark: Position-and-effect orthogonal axes, 1d6 resolution
- FATE: 4 outcomes, narrative ladder
- Genesys/Star Wars FFG: Success/Advantage orthogonal dice axes
- World of Darkness: Dice pool with counting successes
- D&D 5e: Action/bonus/reaction split with resource limits
- 4th edition D&D: Standard/Move/Minor with daily/encounter powers
- Lancer: Move + (2 quick OR 1 full) + free actions/overcharge with NPC asymmetry

**Key Patterns Identified:**
1. Resolution systems trend toward multi-axis (success + advantage independent) or degree counts as systems mature
2. Action economy solutions fall into three categories: hard caps with trade-offs (PF2e), resource-based attrition (D&D 5e), and asymmetric NPC design (Lancer)
3. Documented balance problems cluster around summoning/allies and bonus-action optimization rather than the core structure
4. Systems designed for tactical play (PF2e, 4e) use stricter action economy; systems favoring narrative (FATE, Blades) use orthogonal/partial success mechanics
