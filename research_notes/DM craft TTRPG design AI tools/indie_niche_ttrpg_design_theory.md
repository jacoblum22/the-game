# Indie and Niche TTRPG Design Theory: Pitfalls and Frameworks

Research compiled from indie RPG design sources, published design essays, and community discussions focused on systemic design principles and documented pitfalls.

---

## GNS Theory and Its Role in RPG Design Framework

### Takeaway
GNS theory (Gamism/Narrativism/Simulationism) was Ron Edwards' foundational framework for understanding conflicting design goals in TTRPGs, with the key principle that successful systems focus on only one agenda. Edwards later superseded it with the Big Model due to identified limitations.

### Cited Findings
- **Original Framework**: GNS theory identifies three player agendas: Gamism (winning in the face of adversity), Narrativism (providing engaging story), and Simulationism (maintaining rich, internally consistent fictional worlds) — [Wikipedia: GNS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNS_theory)
- **Core Design Principle**: Edwards posited that "enjoyable RPGs focus on only one of these perspectives and that it is a common mistake in RPG design to try to satisfy all three types" — [GNS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNS_theory)
- **Developer**: Ron Edwards, a game designer and co-founder of The Forge (online community for indie RPG design), developed GNS Theory and later refined it — [Ron Edwards (game designer)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Edwards_(game_designer))
- **Evolution**: Edwards later developed The Big Model (1999–2005) with contributions from other Forge users to formalize and organize GNS theory further — [Role-playing game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_game_theory)
- **The Big Model Structure**: Organizes RPG phenomena into four nested boxes: Ephemera (actual table events), Techniques (governing practices), Exploration (basic act of play), and Social Contract (agreement between real people) — [Big Model](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Big_Model)
- **Big Model Creative Agendas**: Recognizes three kinds of creative agenda—Simulationism ("The Right to Dream"), Gamism, and Narrativism—with emphasis on how different designs privilege different exploration aspects — [Big Model](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Big_Model)

### Inferences
- GNS theory serves as a diagnostic tool for identifying design conflicts: when a system tries to serve all three agendas equally, internal mechanical contradictions often emerge
- The move from GNS to Big Model suggests that early framework had gaps in explaining social contract and practice layers—indicating design frameworks must account for table culture, not just stated mechanics
- For AI-run TTRPGs, the framework helps diagnose whether the system is internally coherent: is it primarily gamist (optimizing for challenge/difficulty tuning), narrativist (curating story beats), or simulationist (maintaining world consistency)?

### Gaps
- No specific case studies found illustrating how GNS theory analysis prevented or corrected design failures in practice
- Limited documentation on how Big Model improves upon GNS for design decision-making (beyond theoretical refinement)

## Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering: Character Build Philosophy

### Takeaway
Robin D. Laws' published work emphasizes that players should build characters with narrative integration and table compatibility as primary design goals, not character power optimization in isolation. Character design is fundamentally a group activity constrained by story needs.

### Cited Findings
- **Character Build Principle**: "The job of players is to build characters that have a need and desire to join in the story, and players shouldn't build characters that veto the characters" — [Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering](https://www.amazon.com/Robins-Laws-Good-Game-Mastering/dp/1556346298); covered in [Four Tips From Robin's Laws](https://slyflourish.com/four_tips_from_robins_laws.html)
- **Designer Background**: Robin D. Laws is a noted game designer (Feng Shui, Hero Wars, Dying Earth, Rune) and Dragon magazine columnist — [Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1785704.Robin_s_Laws_of_Good_Game_Mastering)
- **Core DMing Philosophy**: "Your goal as a DM is to build entertainment, and you should build your game for the fun of your group above all other goals" — [Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering](https://www.amazon.com/Robins-Laws-Good-Game-Mastering/dp/1556346298)
- **Player Type Framework**: Laws identifies distinct player archetypes including the Power Gamer (who wants to maximize character power and sees the PC as "an abstraction, a collection of super powers optimized for the acquisition of still more super powers") and the Butt-Kicker (who wants vicarious mayhem and picks simple, combat-ready characters) — [Four Tips From Robin's Laws](https://slyflourish.com/four_tips_from_robins_laws.html); [Player Types from Robin D. Laws](https://www.darkshire.net/jhkim/rpg/theory/models/robinslaws.html)
- **System Analysis**: Laws advocates that GMs should "Know your players, Analyze your system, Build your campaign, Design adventures, and Prepare to be spontaneous" — [Four Tips From Robin's Laws](https://slyflourish.com/four_tips_from_robins_laws.html)

### Inferences
- Character build design cannot be evaluated in isolation from the table's social agreement—a "powerful" build is bad design if it creates character incompatibility with the story or group dynamics
- Different player types require different character-build constraints: Power Gamers need visible optimization paths, Butt-Kickers need simplicity and combat readiness
- For AI-run TTRPGs, character build systems must include friction that enforces story coherence (e.g., requiring narrative justification for unusual combinations) rather than pure mechanical optimization

### Gaps
- No specific character build examples or case studies documenting which systems Laws considered successful or problematic
- Unclear how Laws' framework handles systems with non-combat or non-narrative-heavy focuses

## Kobold Guide to Game Design: Published Design Essay Frameworks

### Takeaway
The Kobold Guide to Game Design is a 240-page comprehensive collection of 40+ essays by industry designers covering rule creation, magic systems, campaign design, audience understanding, and publishing. It represents distilled practical game design theory from multiple designer perspectives.

### Cited Findings
- **Scope**: The Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design offers "240 pages of in-depth essays on what makes RPGs tick" with "40 essays covering every aspect of RPG design from core rules and magic systems to fantasy adventures, monster design, playtesting, and much more" — [Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design](https://koboldpress.com/take-a-look-at-the-complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design/)
- **Contributors**: Features essays from "game design superstars such as Monte Cook, Ed Greenwood, Michael A. Stackpole, and Keith Baker" — [Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design by Wolfgang Baur](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/17177271-complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design)
- **Coverage Areas**: "Everything from rule creation and setting creation, to campaign design, to understanding your audience, right down to how to craft a pitch for a publisher and how to handle rejection" — [Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design](https://koboldpress.com/kpstore/product/complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design/)
- **Notable Essays**: Michael A. Stackpole's "Designing Magic Systems" and Rob Heinsoo's "Seize the Hook" are highlighted as particularly valuable — [Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design](https://koboldpress.com/kpstore/product/complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design/)
- **Recognition**: Won a 2012 Gold ENnie Award for Best RPG-Related Accessory — [Complete Kobold Guide to Game Design by Wolfgang Baur](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/17177271-complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design)
- **Availability**: Published in multiple formats (paperback, hardcover, PDF, Kindle) — [Complete KOBOLD Guide to Game Design - Kobold Press Store](https://koboldpress.com/kpstore/product/complete-kobold-guide-to-game-design/)

### Inferences
- The presence of essays on "understanding your audience" and "handling rejection" suggests that design theory in the Kobold Guide extends beyond mechanics to include practical commercial and social realities
- The focus on "seizing the hook" (presumed narrative engagement) alongside magic system design indicates these essays treat narrative design and mechanical design as equally critical
- For AI-run TTRPGs, the Kobold Guide's emphasis on audience understanding is particularly relevant—understanding what types of players the system serves prevents over-generalization

### Gaps
- Web search did not locate specific essay titles or summaries beyond the two highlighted essays (Stackpole's magic design, Heinsoo's "Seize the Hook")
- No access to the full text; cannot extract specific design principles beyond the stated essay topics
- Unclear which essays focus specifically on character customization systems vs. other design domains

## Indie Communities and Common Character Customization Mistakes

### Takeaway
Indie RPG design communities identify consistent pitfalls in character customization systems: ability taxes (required-but-optional abilities), systematic option bloat, failure to support iconic character archetypes from fiction, and gear/equipment imbalance. These represent documented design antipatterns.

### Cited Findings
- **Ability Tax Problem**: "Certain abilities are presented as optional but are either so overpowered that you'd be foolish not to take them, or are merely a necessity for your character build to work" — [Seven Common Problems in Character Creation – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)
- **Archetype Incompleteness**: "Games sometimes fail to offer the character options that players expect." Example: "The Iron Kingdoms RPG shares a setting with the Warmachine miniatures game, but players who want to emulate their favorite characters from the fiction often find it impossible, as some prominent characters have abilities that can't be duplicated in the RPG" — [Seven Common Problems in Character Creation – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)
- **Magic System Customization Failure**: "Mage: The Ascension appears to have options for many wizardly archetypes, but it's actually quite difficult to make a classic necromancer, as controlling ghosts and raising zombies requires many different magical abilities that most beginning characters can't accomplish" — [Seven Common Problems in Character Creation – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)
- **Gear Balance Problem**: "Games can create balance problems when gear is more powerful than other character options. Designers sometimes see the risk of losing gear as a balancing factor against inborn abilities, but this logic fails because players dislike losing things they spent points on, leaving GMs in an impossible position between allowing overpowered gear or letting characters become underpowered" — [Seven Common Problems in Character Creation – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)
- **Option Bloat**: "Systemic option bloat...can limit creative character possibilities" — [Seven Common Problems in Character Creation – Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)

### Inferences
- The ability-tax pattern reveals a design error where *optional* mechanics become *mandatory* through power imbalance, forcing players into predetermined builds
- Archetype incompleteness suggests character customization systems must actively audit against fiction (what iconic characters players want to play) rather than deriving options only from mechanical logic
- The gear balance problem illustrates a deeper principle: players experience psychological attachment to acquired resources; mechanical solutions that require losing them will fail emotionally even if they work numerically
- For AI-run TTRPGs, systems need explicit guardrails preventing ability taxes (e.g., no "tax" ability can provide more raw power than a dedicated build path)

### Gaps
- Did not locate specific r/rpgdesign posts or threads; Mythcreants appears to be the primary indie source with documented antipatterns
- No documented case studies of specific indie RPG designs that overcame these problems successfully
- Unclear which of these issues are most common across indie designs vs. which are theoretical edge cases

## Decision Paralysis in Character Customization Systems

### Takeaway
Documented decision/analysis paralysis in character creation occurs when players face too many choices with unclear outcomes, causing the game to halt during character creation. Solutions include simplification paths for casual players, hierarchical choice presentation, and archetype-based templates that reduce initial option space.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition**: "Providing too many choices, or having the outcome of the choices be too complicated, can result in analysis paralysis—a situation where one player spends far too much time trying to make a decision, and grinds the entire game to a halt. Choice Paralysis is a phenomenon where you fail to make a superior choice in a natural time frame: out of fear of making a wrong choice, or being overwhelmed by too many choices" — [D&D 5E (2014) - Too many choices? (Options Paralysis)](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)
- **Character Creation Specificity**: "Character selection screens that present many options with skill points and appearance choices can make it impossible to come to a concrete conclusion in a short time. In many games, what you choose at the start can make the difference between a great game and a rage quit, especially when starting choices influence each subsequent level-up and your end-game situation" — [D&D 5E (2014) - Too many choices? (Options Paralysis)](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)
- **Baldur's Gate 3 Solution**: "Baldur's Gate 3 uses intuitive ways for casual players to speedrun the character sheet if they are looking to play the game rather than focus on the nitty-gritty of their character" — [D&D 5E (2014) - Too many choices? (Options Paralysis)](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)
- **Design Principle**: "Game designers must decide how to segment and present choices so that the player isn't overwhelmed, from character creation to strategy" — [How You as a Developer can Deal With Choice Paralysis in Games](https://medium.com/@acevips/how-you-as-a-developer-can-deal-with-choice-paralysis-in-games-6dc64b00ba21)

### Inferences
- Decision paralysis is not merely a preference for "streamlined" systems—it's a documented game-halting problem. The stakes are high: character creation choices can determine whether a player "rage quits" or engages
- The success of Baldur's Gate 3's approach (offering both detailed and quick-path options) suggests dual-path character creation (expert vs. casual flow) is a viable solution pattern
- For AI-run TTRPGs: decision paralysis is particularly dangerous because the AI cannot directly prompt the human for a faster decision. Systems must have aggressive defaults or strict option-presentation hierarchies
- The relationship between character creation choices and end-game power suggests that character customization *feedback loops* amplify paralysis (a small early mistake cascades through the game)

### Gaps
- No documented theory on *how many choices* trigger paralysis (is there a tested threshold?)
- Limited documentation on how archetype templates or decision-tree approaches compare empirically
- Unclear whether decision paralysis is primarily about absolute number of options or about clarity of option consequences

## Crunch vs. Playability: Balancing Mechanical Depth with Accessibility

### Takeaway
Crunch (mechanical complexity and detailed simulation) is not inherently bad; research distinguishes between *types* of crunch and emphasizes that designers must understand which player types find which complexity levels emotionally satisfying. The solution is flexible systems with optional rules rather than removing complexity entirely.

### Cited Findings
- **Crunch Definition**: "Crunch refers to the complexity and detail of game mechanics. Crunchy games have a number of viable options inside of a complex framework that a skilled player can select that will give them a mechanically favorable outcome" — [Balancing crunch](https://medium.com/rpguide/balancing-crunch-9193faa217f7)
- **Different Types of Crunch**: "Games may have different procedures for different situations (like Errant's procedures for managing estates and legal trials), which can provide deeper immersion" — [Crunch in Roleplaying Games – The Stochastic Game](https://ludovic.chabant.com/blog/2022/10/08/crunch-in-roleplaying-games/)
- **Player Preference Variation**: "Players find certain complexity levels emotionally satisfying, though that level varies even among crunch-lovers. Crunch appeals to particular player types, including butt-kickers, tacticians, and power gamers" — [Balancing crunch](https://medium.com/rpguide/balancing-crunch-9193faa217f7)
- **Design Solution - Flexible Systems**: "Systems can be designed to be customizable with optional rules that provide varying levels of crunchiness, allowing players to add complexity like Fighting Styles and Character Attacks, or leave them out" — [Balancing crunch](https://medium.com/rpguide/balancing-crunch-9193faa217f7)
- **Know Your Players**: "Designers should know what stories their players like, what they enjoy in a game, and how comfortable they are with the rule system, as a system that is too smooth or too crunchy might lose players" — [Balancing crunch](https://medium.com/rpguide/balancing-crunch-9193faa217f7)
- **Mechanics as Tone**: "Mechanics Are Vibes Too: How Rules Shape the Feel of Your TTRPG" (title from 2025 article suggests mechanics directly communicate design intent and tone through their granularity) — [Mechanics Are Vibes Too: How Rules Shape the Feel of Your TTRPG](https://therpggazette.wordpress.com/2025/04/28/mechanics-are-vibes-too-how-rules-shape-the-feel-of-your-ttrpg/)

### Inferences
- Crunch is a tone-setting mechanism, not merely a complexity burden. The *type* of crunch (e.g., tactical positioning detail vs. economic simulation detail) signals what the game cares about and attracts different player types
- The optional-rules approach suggests that a single rulebook can serve multiple audiences if designed with modularity in mind—rather than creating separate "beginner" and "advanced" games
- For AI-run TTRPGs: crunch level should be tunable based on detected player preference, and the AI's internal simulation depth can exceed the *presented* crunch level (deep simulation with simple presentation)

### Gaps
- No empirical data on which types of crunch correlate with decision paralysis (is tactical crunch more paralyzing than economic crunch?)
- Limited case studies showing successful optional-rule systems that don't fragment into two incompatible subgames
- Unclear whether "mechanics are vibes" principle has been formalized into design guidance

## Powered by the Apocalypse Framework: Fiction-First Design Philosophy

### Takeaway
Vincent Baker and Meguey Baker's Apocalypse World introduced a foundational indie design philosophy: "fiction-first" design where mechanics emerge from narrative needs and game feel is embedded in rules presentation. The PbtA framework shifted TTRPG design away from game systems as simulationist rulebooks toward games as collaborative storytelling tools with embedded constraints.

### Cited Findings
- **Designer and Philosophy**: Vincent Baker is "a designer and theorist of tabletop role-playing games and owner of indie role-playing games publisher Lumpley Games. He and his wife Meguey Baker designed Apocalypse World, the first game in the Powered by the Apocalypse system" — [Vincent Baker – lumpley games](https://lumpley.games/author/lumpley/)
- **Design Writing Focus**: "Vincent Baker's game design theory articles highlight how to construct 'Moves,' how to refine a game through iteration, and how to move the themes of a 'PBTA' game away from conflict and towards other transformative experiences" — [Powered by the Apocalypse, part 1 – lumpley games](https://lumpley.games/2019/12/30/powered-by-the-apocalypse-part-1/)
- **Presentation Philosophy**: "Baker writes his game books as if he were writing about games themselves—rules are given explanations and background, the people running the game are given guidelines and principles rather than a full system of rules, and the rules and principles of the game lay out the tone and theme admirably well" — [How the Minds Behind APOCALYPSE WORLD Brought Their Game to a New Generation](https://www.ttrpginsider.news/p/how-the-minds-behind-apocalypse-world-brought-their-game-to-a-new-generation)
- **Legacy and Influence**: "Powered by the Apocalypse, the game design framework created by the Bakers for Apocalypse World, has made a lasting impact on role-playing game design. Apocalypse World has been viewed as 'the beginning of a critical era in 'fiction-first' TRPG design'" — [How the Minds Behind APOCALYPSE WORLD Brought Their Game to a New Generation](https://www.ttrpginsider.news/p/how-the-minds-behind-apocalypse-world-brought-their-game-to-a-new-generation)
- **Generative Framework**: "The game's legacy comes less from the game itself than from the Bakers' invitation to other designers to create their own games inspired by Apocalypse World" — [How the Minds Behind APOCALYPSE WORLD Brought Their Game to a New Generation](https://www.ttrpginsider.news/p/how-the-minds-behind-apocalypse-world-brought-their-game-to-a-new-generation)

### Inferences
- PbtA's "moves" system is a syntax for translating player intent into constrained narrative outcomes; it provides structure without prescribing story beats
- The philosophy of embedding tone in rules presentation suggests that mechanics should be *readable* as design intent—a rule phrasing communicates whether the game values dramatic effect or simulation accuracy
- For AI-run TTRPGs: the PbtA principle that mechanics should enable "transformative experiences" (beyond conflict) suggests the AI's rule application should prioritize emotional/narrative impact over mechanical purity
- The emphasis on "principles rather than full system of rules" aligns with the documented decision-paralysis problem: give players/GMs guidance principles, not exhaustive option lists

### Gaps
- Limited access to specific Baker design essays; the research primarily cites secondary coverage of PbtA philosophy
- No documented analysis of how PbtA systems handle character customization specifically (the framework appears more relevant to narration than build choice)
- Unclear whether PbtA's fiction-first approach has analogous frameworks in simulationist or gamist traditions

## Dungeon World and Archetype-Based Character Design

### Takeaway
Dungeon World adapted the PbtA framework for D&D-style play by replacing class-as-everything with playbook-based archetypes supplemented by modular specialties. This approach addresses the tension between iconic character archetypes and customization, though the system faced criticism for being insufficiently granular for D&D's traditional broad class structure.

### Cited Findings
- **Playbook System**: "In the Dungeon World system, each class has its own playbook that includes a character sheet and all the information required to fill it out such as starting moves, what races, alignments, bond suggestions, etc." — [Character Creation - A Dungeon World Wiki](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Character_Creation)
- **Modular Architecture**: "Dungeon World character classes are broken down into smaller pieces like specialties and archetypes, with players creating starting characters by choosing an archetype and two or three specialties" — [Character Creation - A Dungeon World Wiki](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Character_Creation)
- **Design Guidance**: "When creating classes in Dungeon World, an excellent first step is to think about what fictional characters you'd like to take inspiration from. Additionally, bonds are where the class' outlook shines through and where designers most clearly interact with the player at character creation" — [Creating Classes - A Dungeon World Wiki](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Creating_Classes)
- **Supplement Development**: "Bonds are where the class' outlook shines through" and expanded systems like "Class Warfare" provide "an expanded system of character creation" with further modular options — [Building Characters From Archetypes](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2021/08/11/building-characters-from-archetypes/)
- **Design Limitation**: "Though popular, Dungeon World fell down in the Powered by the Apocalypse pantheon because D&D classes are generally too broad to make effective playbooks" — [Powered by the Apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powered_by_the_Apocalypse)

### Inferences
- The emphasis on "fictional characters you'd like to inspire from" operationalizes the earlier indie principle of archetype support (ensuring icon characters are playable)
- Bonds as a character-creation tool suggest that narrative integration (relationships, party coherence) can be enforced at the mechanical level, not left to GM discretion
- The criticism that "D&D classes are too broad" implies a design principle: effective playbooks are narrower and more thematic than traditional classes, with customization coming from *specialty stacking* rather than within-class option bloat
- For AI-run TTRPGs: the playbook + specialty model is a pattern that constrains both character design and AI narrative response (each playbook has a thematic anchor)

### Gaps
- No detailed analysis of how specialty stacking prevents decision paralysis or ability-tax problems
- Limited documentation on how successful players found workarounds to the "Dungeon World is too narrow" criticism
- Unclear whether the bonds system empirically improves party coherence or is merely a stated design goal

## Feat Bloat, Supplement Creep, and Character Build System Decay

### Takeaway
A documented pitfall in character customization systems is uncontrolled supplement expansion and feat proliferation, which renders earlier options weaker or obsolete, fragmenting player-base optimization strategies. Design controls include clear investment constraints, gatekeeping behind character-build prerequisites, and limiting options per supplement cycle.

### Cited Findings
- **Supplement Bloat Problem**: "Supplement bloat can quickly escalate via new publications, sometimes rendering earlier published feats either reduced in mechanical effectiveness or obsolete" — [Avoiding Ability Bloat](https://forums.rpgmakerweb.com/threads/avoiding-ability-bloat.104656/)
- **Investment Cost Failure**: "Excessive investment for an option is one of the quicker ways to kill viability in that option. This highlights how character building systems can create problems when options require too much resource expenditure to be worthwhile" — [Avoiding Ability Bloat](https://forums.rpgmakerweb.com/threads/avoiding-ability-bloat.104656/)
- **Option Meaningfulness**: "What's the point of a character building option if it does not build their character? This fundamental question drives discussions about whether feats meaningfully impact character development" — [Feat Design - Rhetoric Studios](https://rhetoricstudios.com/cyrad/feat-design/)
- **Modularization Function**: "Feats can help a designer streamline the combat system by modularizing extraneous actions, otherwise the options would either exist as a long list of optional rules or not exist at all" — [Feat Design - Rhetoric Studios](https://rhetoricstudios.com/cyrad/feat-design/)
- **Successful Constraint Model**: "The Without Number series of RPGs handles feats in a manner where players get the most bang for their buck while including sensible constraints and guidelines for further development" — [Feats, Talents, Powers, any game do them right?](https://www.rpgpub.com/threads/feats-talents-powers-any-game-do-them-right.12938/)

### Inferences
- The core problem is *power creep through supplements*, not options-per-se: each new publication introduces stronger options, devaluing older ones and forcing players to track cross-book interactions
- The distinction between feats as *modularization* (clean organization of existing mechanics) vs. feats as *new power vectors* matters: the former avoids bloat, the latter invites it
- For AI-run TTRPGs: supplement management can be automated (AI tracks which options are viable, suggests oldest-still-valid options, avoids power-creep traps), but requires explicit design controls preventing bloat in the first place

### Gaps
- No formal definition of when a supplement cycle creates "too many" options (is it number, mechanical interaction density, or power spread?)
- Limited case studies of games that successfully managed supplement cycles without power creep
- Unclear whether the Without Number approach (mentioned as successful) has been formally documented elsewhere

## Summary: Key Design Principles for Character Customization Systems

Based on the research, the following patterns emerge as critical for AI-run TTRPG character systems:

1. **Coherence over Choice Volume**: Ensure the system focuses on a single design agenda (GNS framework); trying to serve all play styles through option volume backfires
2. **Narrative Gatekeeping**: Require story justification for character builds; this enforces party coherence (Robin's Laws principle)
3. **Prevent Ability Taxes**: Eliminate options that are "optional but mandatory" through power imbalance
4. **Decision-Path Hierarchy**: Provide both novice (template) and expert (modular build) paths; this mitigates decision paralysis without limiting depth
5. **Archetype Coverage**: Audit character system against fictional icons players want to play; ensure core archetypes are viable out-of-box
6. **Crunch as Tone Signal**: Use mechanical complexity to communicate what the game values; tie crunch level to player preference, not universal complexity
7. **Avoid Supplement Bloat**: If character options expand over time, establish clear power ceilings and deprecation policy to prevent power creep
8. **PbtA-style Principles**: Embed design philosophy in rules presentation so constraints feel coherent, not arbitrary
9. **Feedback-Loop Awareness**: Recognize that character creation choices compound through gameplay; small early mistakes can cascade
