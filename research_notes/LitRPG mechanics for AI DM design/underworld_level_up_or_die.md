# Underworld: Level Up or Die (Apollos Thorne) — Progression System Mechanics

**Author confirmation:** The series is written by **Apollos Thorne** (not "Yrsillar" — no evidence found connecting that name to this series). It began as a serial on Royal Road (*Underworld – Level Up or Die!*, royalroad.com/fiction/8397) before commercial publication, and the first novel (*Level Up or Die!*) was published November 6, 2017. The series has since expanded to at least 8–9 books (Book 8 "The Succubi," 2025; Book 9 announced/upcoming as of this research). — [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Underworld-Level-Up-Die-LitRPG/dp/1733664416), [Royal Road](https://www.royalroad.com/fiction/8397/underworld-level-up-or-die), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die), [Risingshadow](https://www.risingshadow.net/book/78331-underworld-book-9)

**Source-quality caveat (read before using this report):** This is a smaller LitRPG series with a thin secondary-source footprint. I found **no fan wiki** (no Fandom wiki exists for this series — searches for one returned only unrelated "Underworld" properties like Ultima Underworld and Underworld Ascendant). I found **no dedicated Reddit/r/litrpg mechanics breakdown** — general searches for Reddit discussion returned no matching threads at all, only Goodreads/Amazon pages. All findings below come from: the LitRPG Podcast database entry, a small progression-fantasy database listing, two independent blog book reviews (Darrienia; Read with Senefer), and Goodreads/Amazon review snippets surfaced via search. None of these sources provide a systematic rules breakdown (no wiki-style stat tables, no skill lists, no formulas) — they are narrative-review-level descriptions. Treat every mechanical claim below as filtered through a reviewer's paraphrase, not a primary-source rules dump, unless otherwise noted.

## How do core stats/attributes work in this system?

### Takeaway
The system uses a conventional D&D-style attribute array — Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom (a "classic" stat-point-allocation LitRPG) — with player-assigned stat points gained from leveling, but no source gave the underlying point-cost formula, caps, or exact display format of the character sheet.

### Cited Findings
- "In the Underworld series, it's a classic LitRPG where the players are able to level up and set their stats as they see fit. The system includes stat attributes such as Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, and Wisdom." — [search-derived synthesis, no single primary URL isolated; corroborated across Goodreads/series pages](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)
- The protagonist (Elorion) "gathers plenty of data to figure out the system before committing stat points or energy to leveling skills" — a deliberate, informed min/max approach rather than blind allocation — [Goodreads review synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die)
- A reviewer of the audiobook edition noted the protagonist allocates heavily into **Wisdom** (as a nominal healer-class stat) but the book's own internal logic undercuts the stat's meaning: "intelligence stat became a bit redundan[t] in this book" because the character learns elemental magic that should logically require Intelligence, not Wisdom — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- The same review describes the in-book stat display as unusually granular/cluttered, listing multiple stat lines as "unknown" repeatedly rather than resolved numeric values — implying the character sheet UI shown on-page has more line-items than the story resolves — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- A separate search surfaced examples of stat modifiers from abilities/items phrased as flat bonuses/penalties, e.g. "+5 Strength, +5 Dexterity, +5 Constitution" or "-49% Wisdom" — suggesting the system supports both flat stat-point bonuses and percentage-based stat penalties/debuffs from effects or equipment. This could not be traced to a specific primary excerpt or single citable URL; flagging as **weakly sourced** (search-engine synthesis, not a quoted passage from a named review).
- One review characterizes the series as having "a huge amount of stat crunchiness, but done in a way that's compelling to read" — again a search-synthesized characterization without a traceable single-article URL; flagging as **weakly sourced**.

### Inferences
- The stat system is attribute-driven (5 classic stats) with level-up point allocation, not a skill-point-only or attribute-less system — this is the "standard" LitRPG chassis rather than a novel stat model.
- The presence of percentage-based debuffs alongside flat bonuses suggests the system tracks both base stat values and modifiers/multipliers separately (a base+modifier stack), which has implications for how complex it would be to adjudicate live (see complexity section below).
- Intelligence appears to be a semi-vestigial/underused stat relative to Wisdom, at least in early books — a potential balance wrinkle rather than a designed redundancy.

### Gaps
- No source gives the actual point-buy costs, per-level point totals, or hard caps for any attribute.
- No source shows an actual verbatim in-book stat block/character sheet (only paraphrased descriptions of one).
- Could not verify the "+5/+5/+5" and "-49% Wisdom" examples against a specific, citable passage or review — these came from an AI-synthesized search summary rather than a page I could independently fetch and confirm. **Do not treat these numbers as confirmed; they are unverified and possibly hallucinated by the search tool's summarization.**

## How does the class system work — is class fixed, or can it change/evolve?

### Takeaway
Elorion's class is **"Blue Mage"** — a specific, named class built around absorbing/copying the abilities of monsters — and reviews describe the author repeatedly *expanding* what that class can do over the course of the series (new magic types, new abilities) rather than showing a clean fixed-class-at-creation model. Whether this is formal "class evolution" (a system-sanctioned class change/tier-up) or just cumulative skill acquisition within one class was not clearly distinguished by any source.

### Cited Findings
- Elorion's class/build is explicitly a **"Blue Mage (Power Copying)"** — a class themed around absorbing the natural skills/abilities of other creatures and mimicking them via magic ("Blue Magic") — [search synthesis corroborated by multiple sources: Apollos Thorne site author page](https://apollos-thorne.com/index.php/author/elorion/), [TV Tropes "Power Copying" trope reference surfaced in the same search](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Bluemage)
- "Blue Magic" as a mechanic is described as "the basic skill that all blue magic is based upon, allowing you to steal the natural skills of other creatures and mimic their abilities through magic" and Elorion "can absorb all the Blue Magic he can find as one of his options for progression" — [search-synthesized from Apollos Thorne site sequel-book description](https://apollos-thorne.com/index.php/author/elorion/)
- A Read with Senefer review states the protagonist is nominally classified as a **healer** but the healing magic he's given "actually hurt everything in the world" it's used in, and the author supplements this with debuffs, buffs, and fire/elemental magic — i.e., the character functions far outside the boundaries of his nominal class label. The reviewer explicitly frames this as the author "abandoning the healer role for flashy elemental magic" and criticizes it as unbalanced given that "traditional healers require groups to be viable" — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- The same review states directly: "the author keep[s] changing it so the main character can learn other kinds of magic and be more overpowered" — describing an author-driven, in-narrative loosening of class boundaries over the course of the book rather than a single one-time evolution event — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- A later-book Goodreads-derived review note (unspecified book, likely a sequel) states: "elorion isnt as overpowered as he was in book 1[,] he just has an ability that is poison to most underworld creatures and uses it in creative ways" — implying his kit keeps gaining new signature abilities book-to-book — [search synthesis, Goodreads reviews](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)
- Other students/characters in the same cohort are shown choosing different classes/specializations at the start (20 kidnapped students "had to devise how best to level up, whether to team up, and what classes to focus on") — confirming multiple distinct classes exist among the cast, not just Elorion's — [search synthesis, Fantastic Fiction / Goodreads](https://www.fantasticfiction.com/t/apollos-thorne/level-up-or-die.htm)

### Inferences
- Class is **not rigidly fixed at character creation** in a way that walls off ability access — the Blue Mage's entire premise is porous class boundaries (steal/absorb abilities from monsters), and the narrative shows the author continually widening what the class does. This reads less like a formal "class change/tier-up" mechanic (e.g. "Novice Healer → Adept Healer") and more like an inherently open-ended class whose growth path is "whatever abilities you successfully copy," which is itself the signature hook (see below).
- The "healer who doesn't heal, but has hostile/damaging healing magic" is a deliberate subversion built into the class design, not a bug — but reviewers treat its downstream balance effects (removing the healer's group-dependency weakness) as a real balance problem.

### Gaps
- No source describes a formal class-tier/evolution system (e.g., named tiers, a class-change quest/ritual, or system-granted "class evolved" notifications) as distinct from ability acquisition. It's unclear whether the book ever presents a discrete class-change moment or whether "Blue Mage" is Elorion's class for the entire series with organically expanding abilities.
- No source lists what other classes exist in the setting besides "Blue Mage" and "healer," beyond the vague statement that the 20 students chose different classes.
- No confirmation of whether classes can be freely reassigned/respecced by any character, or whether Elorion's case (a Blue Mage effectively also becoming a healer/elementalist) is an exception unique to his ability rather than a general system rule.

## How does leveling and skill acquisition work?

### Takeaway
Leveling is standard XP/kill-and-challenge-driven level-up with stat points awarded per level, but the series is explicitly noted (by both critical and positive reviewers) for **very rapid, very high protagonist level growth relative to the wider cast and even relative to the setting's own scale** — with sources citing concrete numbers (starting at level 25, reaching level 57 within the audiobook's first hour, defeating a level 200 enemy while still under level 100) and describing an explicitly uncapped, high-ceiling setting ("no level limit," enemies "in the thousands and tens of thousands" in later books).

### Cited Findings
- The Read with Senefer review gives specific, quotable numbers: characters "begin at level 25"; the protagonist reaches "level 57 within the first hour of the audiobook"; he then "defeat[s] a level 200 enemy while still under level 100." The reviewer explicitly calls this "severely unbalanced." — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- A different reviewer (paraphrased from Goodreads search results) states the protagonist "becomes super OP way too fast, outleveling all other players[,] and kills a super strong boss by chapter 6-7" — corroborating extremely fast early-book leveling as a recurring critical complaint — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die)
- Skill/magic acquisition for Elorion specifically runs through the Blue Mage mechanic: killing or otherwise absorbing monsters grants their "Blue Magic" abilities directly, rather than skills being purchased with points or trained via trainers/books as the sole route — [Apollos Thorne author site, search synthesis](https://apollos-thorne.com/index.php/author/elorion/)
- General play loop per the LitRPG Podcast review: "fights monster after monster to grind skill and magic levels," with spells/skills apparently tiered novice → intermediate → advanced ("Spells and some skills start at novice level and move from intermediate to advance") — [LitRPG Podcast database review](http://litrpgpodcast.com/litrpg-database/2017/11/9/underworld-level-up-or-die)
- By later books, the setting is described (in a positive review) as having "no level limit and uber high level bad guys (in the thousands and tens of thousands)," with "level gain and stats much higher than in most LitRPGs" — indicating the numeric scale of levels/stats escalates dramatically as the series progresses — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)
- Late-series content adds non-combat progression avenues: "a little town building and crafting" appear "towards the end" of at least one book — [LitRPG Podcast database review](http://litrpgpodcast.com/litrpg-database/2017/11/9/underworld-level-up-or-die)

### Inferences
- The skill/spell tiering (novice → intermediate → advanced) implies skills level up in place with use/practice, in addition to (or instead of) being unlocked purely by spending points — consistent with "grinding" being the explicit description of play.
- The jump from "level 25 start, level 57 in-hour" to "enemies in the tens of thousands of levels" in later books signals the level scale itself is not stable across the series — the author appears to have re-scaled the power/level economy upward as the series progressed (see power creep section).

### Gaps
- No source specifies the XP curve, how many stat points are awarded per level, or whether skill "levels" (novice/intermediate/advanced) are a separate track from character level.
- No source explains the actual mechanic by which Blue Magic is "absorbed" (on kill? on hit? requires a ritual/cooldown? success chance?) — only that it happens.
- No confirmation of whether non-Blue-Mage characters acquire skills differently (trainers, skill books, quest rewards) — all detail here is Elorion-specific.

## Is there a distinctive hook to this series' system?

### Takeaway
Yes — the clear, repeatedly-confirmed signature mechanic is the **"Blue Mage" power-copying/absorption system**: the protagonist's class is explicitly built around stealing/mimicking the natural abilities of the monsters he encounters (an LitRPG implementation of the "Blue Mage" archetype familiar from Final Fantasy), making his build a running tally of absorbed monster abilities rather than a fixed spell list chosen from a class tree.

### Cited Findings
- Elorion's class is named and tagged directly as **"Blue Mage (Power Copying)"** across multiple independent search-surfaced sources — [Apollos Thorne site](https://apollos-thorne.com/index.php/author/elorion/), corroborated by [TV Tropes' "Power Copying" reference appearing alongside it in search results](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/Main/Bluemage)
- The mechanic is described concretely as: "Blue Magic... allow[s] you to steal the natural skills of other creatures and mimic their abilities through magic," with Elorion able to "absorb all the Blue Magic he can find as one of his options for progression" — [search synthesis of Apollos Thorne site content](https://apollos-thorne.com/index.php/author/elorion/)
- "Blue Mage" appears as a recurring **tag** across multiple book entries in the LitRPG Podcast's database for this author, confirming it's a through-line of the whole series rather than a one-book gimmick — [LitRPG Podcast database, Apollos Thorne tag page](http://litrpgpodcast.com/litrpg-database/tag/Apollos+Thorne)
- A later-book review note describes Elorion using "an ability that is poison to most underworld creatures... in creative ways" as his standout tool in a subsequent book — consistent with a build defined by an accumulating, idiosyncratic toolkit of absorbed/unique abilities rather than a standard class kit — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)

### Inferences
- This hook is the strongest, most game-able differentiator in the series for an AI-DM adaptation: it reframes "build customization" as **"what have you successfully killed/absorbed and what did it give you,"** which is a resource/loot-table problem rather than a talent-tree problem. That is a meaningfully different adjudication burden than a fixed-class game (the GM needs a live registry of "what abilities exist in the bestiary" and "which of those are Blue-Magic-copyable"), discussed further below.

### Gaps
- No source explains the *rules* of absorption: is it automatic on kill, does it require a resource cost, is there a limit to how many abilities can be held/active at once, can abilities be lost, is there a risk/failure state? This is the single biggest mechanical gap in available sources, and it's the piece most load-bearing for an AI-DM implementation.

## What makes builds feel distinct/personalized rather than templated?

### Takeaway
Personalization in this series comes almost entirely from the **Blue Mage absorption hook** (each character's kit is a personal history of what they've killed/copied, not a menu choice) plus explicit **deliberate, informed min-maxing** by the protagonist — reviewers repeatedly frame Elorion as a "genius solo power leveler" who researches the system before spending resources, rather than a character following a templated class path.

### Cited Findings
- Elorion is characterized as engaging "internal discussions about how to best build his character" and "gathers plenty of data to figure out the system before committing stat points or energy to leveling skills" — an explicit in-fiction min-max/optimizer approach — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die)
- A critical review frames this optimizer approach negatively, describing the character as having "no real depth... beyond being a 'genius solo power leveler'" who wins "with ease and without real challenges" — i.e., some readers see the optimization-driven build as making the character feel less personalized/human, not more — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die)
- The healer-who-doesn't-heal build (Blue Mage class nominally slotted as "healer," but who wields hostile healing magic plus debuffs/buffs/fire magic) is cited as a concrete example of a build that diverges sharply from its class label — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- Other students in the kidnapped cohort chose different classes/foci from the outset, implying builds diverge across characters based on class choice + what they individually encounter/absorb, not a single shared build path — [Fantastic Fiction/Goodreads, search synthesis](https://www.fantasticfiction.com/t/apollos-thorne/level-up-or-die.htm)

### Inferences
- The series' idea of "personalization" is closer to *emergent* (what monsters you happened to fight and successfully copy from) than *chosen* (picking from a fixed talent tree) — which is thematically the opposite of most stat-allocation LitRPGs' personalization model, and is the thing most worth stealing for an AI-DM design if the goal is emergent, table-specific builds.

### Gaps
- No concrete example was found of two different characters' builds compared side by side in a review (e.g., "Character A copied fire magic and became a blaster, Character B copied stealth and became an assassin"). All personalization evidence is about Elorion alone; the breadth of build diversity across the wider cast is asserted but never illustrated with specifics in any source I could reach.

## Does the series show power creep or balance issues as it progresses?

### Takeaway
Yes, clearly and on both the critical and positive sides of reviews: this series is repeatedly described as intentionally, dramatically overpowered-protagonist-driven, with numeric level/power scale visibly inflating from "level 25 start / level 200 boss" in book 1 to "no level limit... enemies in the thousands and tens of thousands" in later books — and multiple reviewers explicitly use the word "unbalanced."

### Cited Findings
- Book 1 numbers: start at level 25, protagonist hits level 57 within an hour (audiobook), defeats a level 200 enemy while under level 100 — reviewer explicitly calls this "severely unbalanced" — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- A separate critical review: protagonist "becomes super OP way too fast, outleveling all other players and kills a super strong boss by chapter 6-7," with "everything the character wants to accomplish... done with ease and without real challenges" — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/36483019-level-up-or-die)
- A positive review of a later book frames the escalation as a feature, not a bug: "an overpowered book with no level limit and uber high level bad guys (in the thousands and tens of thousands)," explicitly noting "level gain and stats [are] much higher than in most LitRPGs" — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)
- The Read with Senefer review directly attributes the escalation to authorial intervention rather than systemic/emergent causes: "the author keep[s] changing it so the main character can learn other kinds of magic and be more overpowered" — framing power creep as a deliberate writing choice to keep unlocking new toys for the protagonist, not simply a level-treadmill effect — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)
- A later-book reviewer note suggests some self-correction: in a subsequent book "Elorion isn't as overpowered as he was in book 1[;] he just has an ability that is poison to most underworld creatures and uses it in creative ways" — implying at least one later book dials back raw stat dominance in favor of a narrower signature tool — [Goodreads reviews, search synthesis](https://www.goodreads.com/series/241763-underworld)

### Inferences
- This series sits at the high-power-creep end of the LitRPG spectrum even by genre standards — reviewers who like the series explicitly cite the extreme scale as the selling point ("much higher than most LitRPGs"), while reviewers who dislike it cite the same extreme scale as removing stakes/tension. For an AI-DM design, this is a useful negative example: it shows what happens when there's no soft cap or opposing scaling — trivial-feeling encounters and a snowballing "video-game numbers as spectacle" tone rather than sustained tension.

### Gaps
- No source gives a clean book-by-book power/level table across all 8-9 books; the numeric data points available are anecdotal (book 1 numbers, and one vague later-book comparative note).
- Unclear whether the world's overall level scale (NPCs, other players, monsters) escalated in tandem with the protagonist, or whether the protagonist specifically outpaced a static world — the "outleveling all other players" complaint suggests the latter, at least in book 1.

## Complexity of tracking/adjudicating this system live (human or AI GM) vs. how it reads on the page

### Takeaway
No source directly discusses table-top adjudication or GM-facing complexity (this question is outside what any available review addresses), but the mechanical shape described — an open-ended "copy any monster ability you kill" class layered on classic 5-stat allocation, uncapped levels, and stat displays reviewers call excessively granular/cluttered — implies this system would be **significantly harder to adjudicate live than a fixed-class LitRPG**, because the GM effectively needs an open bestiary-to-ability mapping maintained on the fly rather than a closed, pre-authored skill list.

### Cited Findings
- No review or source directly addresses "how hard would this be to run as a tabletop/GM-adjudicated system" — this is a genuine, total gap in available secondary sources. Flagging explicitly per instructions rather than speculating as fact.
- The one concrete textual detail bearing on UI/complexity: a reviewer describes the in-book character-stat display as showing repeated "unknown" values across multiple stat lines, i.e., the on-page character sheet has more line items than the story ever resolves into concrete numbers — [Read with Senefer review](https://readwithseneferliteraturehouse.home.blog/2021/08/15/level-up-or-die-by-apollos-thorne/)

### Inferences (explicitly flagged as inference, not sourced fact)
- **Open-ended ability acquisition (Blue Mage) is the main complexity driver.** A GM running this system live would need either (a) a pre-built table of "which monster abilities are copyable and what they do" prepared before play, or (b) a fast on-the-fly ability-generation process, since the player's build isn't drawn from a closed list the GM can memorize once — every new monster encountered is a potential new permanent player ability. This is a materially heavier adjudication burden than a talent-tree class system, where the GM only needs to know the finite tree.
- **Uncapped levels with dramatically escalating numeric scale (tens of thousands) would be hard to keep meaningful at a live table** — large bare numbers (level 8,000 vs level 8,050) stop being legible as felt difficulty differences to a human or AI GM improvising encounter difficulty, unless the system is translated into banded tiers for actual play rather than tracked as raw integers.
- **The classic 5-stat + flat/percentage modifier stack** (if the unverified "+5/+5/+5" / "-49% Wisdom" pattern found in search synthesis is accurate) implies a base-value-plus-modifiers bookkeeping model, which is a familiar and very trackable pattern for a GM (same shape as D&D-style buffs/debuffs) — this part would *not* be unusually hard to run live, in contrast to the open-ended ability acquisition.
- On the page, all of this complexity is invisible/frictionless to the reader because the author can simply narrate "Elorion absorbed the [name] ability" and show a status-panel readout without needing to actually simulate consequences in real time or defend against player-side exploitation — a luxury a live GM (human or AI) does not have. This gap between "reads smoothly in prose" and "must be adjudicated fairly and consistently at a table" is the single most important design takeaway from this series for the requesting project, even though no source states it explicitly — it is an inference from the mechanical shape described above, not a cited claim.

### Gaps
- No source discusses actual play, tabletop adaptation, or GM/adjudication complexity for this system at all — everything in this subsection beyond the "unknown stat lines" citation is inference, clearly labeled as such per the research constraints.
- No confirmed mechanic for limits on Blue Magic capacity (how many abilities can be held, whether there's a resource cost or cooldown, whether copying can fail) — without this, it's impossible to say definitively whether the live system is "unbounded" (hard to adjudicate) or actually has hidden caps/costs (more tractable) not mentioned in any review.

## Overall source-coverage note for the report writer

This is a genuinely under-documented series relative to bigger LitRPG names (e.g., no wiki, no dedicated subreddit threads found, no rules-summary blog posts). The most concrete, quotable data points obtained are:
1. Starting level 25 / early rapid leveling to 57 / beating a level-200 enemy under level 100 (book 1).
2. The "Blue Mage" class = steal/mimic monster abilities ("Blue Magic") as the central, series-spanning hook.
3. Classic 5-stat array (STR/DEX/CON/INT/WIS), with Wisdom apparently overloaded relative to Intelligence in early books.
4. Later books escalate to "no level limit," enemies in the "thousands and tens of thousands."
5. Multiple independent reviewers (positive and negative) agree the series is unusually high-power/loose on internal balance, and that this is a deliberate authorial choice (the "healer with hostile healing magic" example, and "the author keeps changing it so the main character can... be more overpowered").

Anything beyond these five points in the sections above is either explicitly marked as weakly-sourced/unverified or is an inference clearly labeled as such. No primary-source excerpt (actual book text) was directly retrieved in this research pass — everything is filtered through reviewer paraphrase, which is a meaningful limitation the report writer should carry forward.
