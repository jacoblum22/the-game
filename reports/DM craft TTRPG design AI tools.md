# Engineering spotlight balance for an AI GM

The single most consequential finding of this third research pass is that the two human-GM techniques this project cares most about — **spotlight balance** and **challenge diversification** — are real, named, widely-cited crafts with concrete operational content, not vague "good GMing" vibes, and both are things an AI GM cannot get for free from good mechanical design; they must be built as explicit scene-framing and encounter-generation logic. Spotlight balance means "everyone gets their turns in the sun across the arc of the campaign," operationalized in practice as 10–15 minute scene rotation and encounter-type variety rather than equal per-session airtime ([Gooey Cube](https://shop.gooeycube.com/blogs/news/tabletop-rpg-adventures-spotlight-management-tabletop-rpgs)), while challenge diversification means responding to an optimized build not by escalating monster stats but by varying the *kind* of problem — environmental, tactical, objective-based, resource-management — so no single specialization dominates every scene ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2055/roleplaying-games/fetishizing-balance); [Scroll for Initiative](https://scrollforinitiative.com/2022/12/04/how-to-make-dd-challenging-at-higher-levels/)). This pass also stress-tested four candidate mechanics — degrees-of-success resolution, action economy design, audited point-buy costs, and move/verb-based abilities — with the same rigor as any other candidate, and the results sharpen rather than overturn the two prior reports' conclusions: closed combinatorial primitives, hidden variance layers, and additive-stacking-without-retirement as the universal failure mode. The new evidence adds a fourth: **unaudited shared-currency customization is its own distinct failure mode**, documented independently in GURPS's point-buy exploits, D&D 5e's feat "ability tax" problem, and 13th Age's Icon Connection system ballooning from three pages to sixty. And the AI-tools landscape confirms the three-layer function-calling-plus-externalized-state-plus-judge-gate architecture as the field's converging answer, while surfacing a genuinely new, still-unsolved problem: no benchmark or paper anywhere addresses fairness in adjudicating a player's novel, rules-unanticipated action, which remains this project's most exposed open frontier.

## Spotlight balance and challenge diversification are craft, not intuition

Robin D. Laws' foundational GMing thesis — "if your group is having fun, then you are a good GM" — sounds like a platitude until it is unpacked into his actual toolkit: seven distinct player archetypes (power gamer, butt-kicker, tactician, specialist, method actor, storyteller, casual gamer), each with different goals a GM should track and design toward, formalized as a "Player Goal Chart" documenting archetype, goal, and specific planned actions per player ([Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/four_tips_from_robins_laws.html)). This is the connective tissue between "spotlight balance" as a slogan and spotlight balance as an engineerable process: Laws is explicit that GMs should "recognize these preferences and actively design experiences addressing each player's motivations," which for an AI GM means modeling player type explicitly rather than assuming one campaign style serves everyone. The Alexandrian's treatment of spotlight balance names the actual failure condition precisely — performance disparities arise either when "two characters occupy the same combat niche" (spotlight competition) or when "campaign style doesn't match a character's specialization" — and the prescribed fix is consistently DM-side flexibility (varying enemy types, encounter frequency, and challenge mix) rather than nerfing the character ([The Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/2055/roleplaying-games/fetishizing-balance)). Concretely, that fix decomposes into techniques an AI GM can implement as rules rather than intuitions: rotate scene focus on a roughly 10–15 minute cadence, and use encounters that intrinsically require multiple players' input — skill challenges being the paradigm case, since they "require input from multiple players" by construction rather than by GM favoritism ([Gooey Cube](https://shop.gooeycube.com/blogs/news/tabletop-rpg-adventures-spotlight-management-tabletop-rpgs)). Crucially, the balancing unit is the campaign arc, not the session: "every player will have fewer turns in the spotlight [in any given session]... the goal is to ensure all players feel valued over the course of the campaign rather than in every individual session," which is a multi-session accounting problem — exactly the kind of longitudinal tracking an AI GM's externalized state store is well-suited to own, but which no source in either this pass or the prior one shows any existing AI-GM system actually implementing.

Challenge diversification is the mirror-image technique for the optimizer problem this project has already flagged via the Alexandrian's "Optimization Arms Race" essay in the prior report, and this pass supplies the concrete tactical vocabulary underneath that essay's abstract recommendation. The named levers are: environmental and tactical variety (lava floors, chokepoints, ranged-favoring open terrain — "four ogres on a road is a very different challenge than four ogres on a rope bridge over lava") ([Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/lazy_approach_to_stronger_monsters.html)); intelligent monster tactics rather than raw stat inflation, since "even low-level enemies like goblins and grimlocks can be surprisingly challenging when played intelligently" ([Scroll for Initiative](https://scrollforinitiative.com/2022/12/04/how-to-make-dd-challenging-at-higher-levels/)); explicit action-economy manipulation, where "if you want to scale up difficulty, tilt the action economy away from the players" by adding bodies rather than raising individual monster power ([Scroll for Initiative](https://scrollforinitiative.com/2022/12/04/how-to-make-dd-challenging-at-higher-levels/)); objective-based encounters that replace "defeat the monster" with "complete the ritual, protect the hostages, avoid the alarm" ([Scroll for Initiative](https://scrollforinitiative.com/2022/12/04/how-to-make-dd-challenging-at-higher-levels/)); resource-pressure narrative framing to prevent the long-rest reset that trivializes high-level play; and encounter mixing — "roll twice on a random encounter table and mix the results together... two groups fighting each other" — as a cheap way to add complications without new content ([Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/lazy_approach_to_stronger_monsters.html)). All of this sits inside a broader pitfall taxonomy this pass confirms independently: railroading ("forcing your story" instead of building situations for players to navigate) and GM-as-adversary (treating combat competitively rather than facilitating character success) are the two most-cited anti-patterns, and the corrective in both cases is the same philosophical stance — "DMs don't create the story, the group does" ([Sly Flourish](https://slyflourish.com/dm_mistakes.html)). The load-bearing gap this pass surfaces, and which no source anywhere closes, is translation: every one of these techniques assumes a human GM's felt sense of "the table is steamrolling" or "the rogue hasn't had a moment in three sessions," and current AI-GM research explicitly flags "delayed responses and limited emotional engagement" as documented weaknesses without offering a substitute for reading the room ([Medium](https://medium.com/@dmckeeve_70751/the-dawn-of-ai-in-tabletop-rpgs-revolutionizing-the-game-master-s-role-d150f6046bb7)) — meaning this project cannot treat spotlight balance and challenge diversification as emergent byproducts of good mechanical design; they need to be first-class, trackable, explicitly-invoked systems (a per-player spotlight ledger, a challenge-type rotation rule) rather than assumed GM instincts.

## Indie design theory names the exact traps this project is trying to avoid

Ron Edwards' GNS theory supplies a coherence diagnostic worth taking seriously even in its simplified form: Gamism (winning against adversity), Narrativism (engaging story), and Simulationism (internally consistent world) are three separable design agendas, and Edwards' central claim is that "enjoyable RPGs focus on only one of these... and it is a common mistake in RPG design to try to satisfy all three types" equally ([Wikipedia: GNS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNS_theory)). Edwards later folded this into the Big Model, adding layers for Techniques, Exploration, and Social Contract specifically because the original framework under-theorized table culture and practice ([RPG Museum: Big Model](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Big_Model)) — the actionable takeaway for this project is a forcing question, not a theorem: is the system being built primarily optimizing for challenge/difficulty tuning, for curated story beats, or for world-consistency simulation, because trying to be all three without prioritization is the documented failure pattern. Robin's Laws contributes a second, sharper constraint specifically on character-build philosophy: "the job of players is to build characters that have a need and desire to join in the story, and players shouldn't build characters that veto the [group's story]" ([slyflourish.com](https://slyflourish.com/four_tips_from_robins_laws.html)) — character optimization divorced from narrative integration is treated as a design failure at the table level, not merely a min-maxing quirk to tolerate.

The most directly useful material in this section, though, is the indie community's documented catalogue of character-customization antipatterns, because each has a crisp, transferable definition. **Ability tax** is "certain abilities... presented as optional but are either so overpowered that you'd be foolish not to take them, or are merely a necessity for your character build to work" ([Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)) — this is the same disease documented independently in the major-systems research below (D&D 5e's Sharpshooter/Great Weapon Master feats) and gives this project a concrete audit rule: no nominally-optional option should be so dominant that skipping it is a mistake. **Archetype incompleteness** is the failure to support iconic characters players actually want to play — the cited case is the Iron Kingdoms RPG, which shares a setting with a miniatures game but where "players who want to emulate their favorite characters from the fiction often find it impossible" because the RPG's mechanics can't reproduce those characters' signature abilities ([Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)) — for a system built explicitly on LitRPG-genre power fantasies, this is a direct audit checklist: can the mechanics actually produce the archetypes the genre promises? **Gear balance failure** is the specific trap where equipment outclasses innate abilities, and the failure mode is psychological, not mathematical: "players dislike losing things they spent points on, leaving GMs in an impossible position between allowing overpowered gear or letting characters become underpowered" ([Mythcreants](https://mythcreants.com/blog/seven-common-problems-in-character-creation/)) — a warning this project should heed if any AI-DM-generated loot economy is on the table. **Option bloat** rounds out the set as the generic version of the additive-stacking failure this project's prior two reports already flagged, but confirmed here from an entirely separate design-community lineage rather than just LitRPG-novel case studies.

Decision paralysis turns out to be a documented, game-halting phenomenon rather than a minor UX complaint: "providing too many choices, or having the outcome of the choices be too complicated, can result in analysis paralysis... a situation where one player spends far too much time trying to make a decision, and grinds the entire game to a halt" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)), and the stakes compound because early character-creation choices cascade through every subsequent level-up and the whole end-game, so "what you choose at the start can make the difference between a great game and a rage quit" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)). The one concretely cited mitigation pattern — Baldur's Gate 3's dual-path character creation, letting casual players "speedrun the character sheet" while still exposing full depth to players who want it ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/too-many-choices-options-paralysis.668893/page-4)) — is directly portable: an AI GM has no equivalent of a human GM saying "just pick something, we'll adjust," so it needs an aggressive-default fast path baked in structurally, not improvised. Crunch-versus-playability research adds a second reframe worth internalizing: crunch is not simply "more complexity is worse," it is a **tone signal** — "crunch appeals to particular player types, including butt-kickers, tacticians, and power gamers" ([Medium: Balancing crunch](https://medium.com/rpguide/balancing-crunch-9193faa217f7)) — and the proposed solution across sources is modular optional-rule layers rather than one universal complexity dial, meaning an AI GM's internal simulation can run deeper than what it exposes to a given player, tuned per-player rather than system-wide. Finally, Dungeon World's playbook-plus-specialty architecture is the one concrete real-world case this pass finds of a system explicitly designed to avoid both archetype incompleteness and decision paralysis simultaneously — players "create starting characters by choosing an archetype and two or three specialties" rather than picking from within one broad, option-bloated class ([A Dungeon World Wiki](https://a-dungeon-world.fandom.com/wiki/Character_Creation)) — though the same source notes the system's own documented weakness, that "D&D classes are generally too broad to make effective playbooks" ([Wikipedia: Powered by the Apocalypse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powered_by_the_Apocalypse)), meaning the playbook-narrowing trick only works if the underlying archetypes are kept genuinely narrow and thematic rather than generic.

## Major systems' 2024-era balance fixes widen the gap they targeted

D&D 5e's 2024 revision is the clearest available case study of a well-resourced, deliberate fix failing on its own terms, and it is worth taking seriously precisely because it shows that *good intentions plus real mechanical changes* are not sufficient without holistic auditing. The pre-2024 problem was well-documented: martial-caster disparity "is the system's most persistent balance issue" ([RPGBot](https://rpgbot.net/2024-dnd/2024-dnd-meta/)), Sharpshooter and Great Weapon Master "feel mandatory" for martial viability while feats like Athlete and Grappler are "rarely worth taking" ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/dnd-broken-official-rules-ban-dungeons-dragons/)) — a textbook ability-tax case matching the Mythcreants framework in the prior section, independently confirmed from a completely different source lineage. The 2024 fix added Weapon Mastery traits to every martial weapon and doubled healing-spell dice ([RPGBot](https://rpgbot.net/2024-dnd/2024-dnd-meta/)), yet the documented outcome is that "new and updated spells made spellcasters even more powerful," with specific spells like Conjure Minor Elementals becoming "game-breaking" and letting "solo Wizards... vastly outpace optimized martial characters" — the sourced verdict is blunt: "the martial-caster divide persists" ([RPGBot](https://rpgbot.net/2024-dnd/2024-dnd-meta/)). The mechanism of failure matters more than the outcome: WotC fixed the martial side of the ledger without re-auditing the caster side against the same standard, which is exactly the "unaudited shared power budget" failure this pass's audited-cost deep-dive names explicitly below — balance work that touches only one arm of an asymmetric system reliably fails, because the other arm keeps moving.

Pathfinder 2e is the sourced counter-case, and its designers were explicit that avoiding "trap options" was a first-order design goal rather than an afterthought: "if you have to spend a great deal of time creating a character or reading a manual, that's a time investment that you have to sink in before the fun can begin," and character options were built to be "proper choices, not a hidden subgame of 'avoid making an incompetent character'" ([Paizo forums](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs2v2wu?Pathfinder-2-has-the-right-design-philosophy=)). The mechanical lever was proficiency math rather than feat curation: "everything a character is proficient in increases by 1 with every level, with additional boosts for Expertise, Mastery, and more" ([CBR](https://www.cbr.com/pathfinder-vs-dnd-5e/)), paired with a deliberate decision to scale back spell power and buff non-magical combat simultaneously — auditing both arms of the asymmetry at once, unlike 5e's 2024 revision ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/pathfinder-2e-dungeons-dragons-5e-better-worse/)). Community reception credits this as a genuine improvement over both PF1e and 5e on the specific martial-caster axis, but names the explicit cost: "critics worried the design sacrifices the tactical depth... veteran players valued" for a system where designers deliberately kept "everyone... largely incapable of accomplishing anything of actual value alone" so no single build can "carry the fight," which some players read as a loss of "feeling of power" ([Paizo forums](https://paizo.com/threads/rzs2v2wu?Pathfinder-2-has-the-right-design-philosophy=)). This is a real tradeoff this project needs to make consciously rather than by accident: tight, exhaustively-audited math buys balance at the cost of some subjective potency.

13th Age contributes two lessons in opposite directions. First, a genuine success story in responsive design: the 1st-edition Ranger's problem was structural rather than a numbers-tuning miss — "abilities that were always available... had to be terrible" for balance purposes, so players found the class dull next to a flexible Wizard, and the 2nd edition fix converted static always-on abilities into "really interesting abilities that do a lot more damage" by making them conditional/active rather than passive ([War Gamer](https://www.wargamer.com/13th-age/2nd-edition-dnd-designers)) — a transferable design rule: passive, always-available abilities are structurally forced to be weak, so build diversity should be built from active, situational triggers rather than passive stat bonuses. Second, a genuine cautionary tale about creep in the *rules-clarity* dimension rather than the power dimension: the Icon Connection system's original rules were "too vague," and even experienced GMs converged on ad hoc house interpretations, forcing the 2nd edition to expand the section "from three pages to approximately 60 pages of detailed examples and clarification" ([War Gamer](https://www.wargamer.com/13th-age/2nd-edition-dnd-designers)) — this is the same additive-stacking-without-retirement failure mode the prior two reports identified in LitRPG novels' character sheets, now confirmed inside a published, professionally-edited TTRPG's *rules text itself*, not just its character-building math. Finally, this pass finds essentially no direct evidence that any major-system designer has treated AI-GM adjudicability as a design criterion at all — the one sourced community discussion notes "no system has been tested at scale... with AI-GMs running the complete ruleset," and flags that bounded-accuracy systems (constant, small bonus ranges) are plausibly more AI-friendly than deeply layered proficiency-tier systems like PF2e simply because they are easier for a model to track, though this remains an untested inference rather than a documented finding ([myarchivist.ai](https://www.myarchivist.ai/ai-dungeon-master/ai-in-ttrpgs)).

## Four candidate mechanics, deep-dived with equal rigor

**Degrees-of-success resolution** is the strongest fit of the four for an AI GM specifically because it is deterministic by construction. Pathfinder 2e's four-degree system computes outcome purely from margin: beating the DC by 10+ is a critical success, missing by 10+ is a critical failure, and a natural 20 or 1 shifts the result one degree regardless of the numeric margin — a fully mechanical rule an adjudicator applies rather than judges ([Archives of Nethys](https://2e.aonprd.com/Rules.aspx?ID=2286)). Other systems solve the same "binary pass/fail is too blunt" problem with different geometries worth comparing directly. FATE's four-outcome ladder (fail/tie/succeed/succeed-with-style) is built around the specific insight that a *tie* needs its own non-punitive resolution so an even roll doesn't stall play — on a tie, "the player typically gets what they want but at a minor cost" ([Fate Core SRD](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/four-outcomes)). Blades in the Dark separates the axis entirely differently: Position (controlled/risky/desperate — "how bad can things go?") and Effect (limited/standard/great — "how much will this action achieve?") are set by the GM *before* the roll, independent of each other, producing nine possible fictional framings before dice even hit the table ([Blades in the Dark SRD](https://bladesinthedark.com/setting-position-effect)). Genesys/Star Wars goes further still by making success/failure and advantage/threat fully orthogonal symbol pools that can partially cancel, so a roll can produce "success with a complication" or "failure with an unexpected benefit" — outcomes a single success-degree number cannot express ([Cannibal Halfling Gaming](https://cannibalhalflinggaming.com/2020/09/16/genesys-in-depth/)). World of Darkness achieves gradation through dice-pool counting instead of thresholds: successes accumulate per die that beats a target number, so degree-of-success scales naturally with character power without any additional resolution rule ([William Pees](https://enantiodromos.substack.com/p/world-of-darkness-dice-pools)). The tradeoff is explicit and well-documented on both sides: degree systems "create opportunities for succeed-at-a-cost mechanics that can be more interesting than just binary outcomes," and "the most interesting aspects of narrative lie in the sweet spot of partial success" ([RPG Museum](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Degree_of_success); [Capacle](https://capacle.substack.com/p/the-cost-of-partial-success)), but they cost more computation per roll and (in Genesys/BitD's case) require the GM to pre-set framing variables the AI would need to generate itself. For this project, PF2e's threshold-margin approach is the standout candidate: it needs zero GM judgment calls, generalizes cleanly to "did the improvised action work," and its only documented cost is that "tight math... makes individual bonuses feel less impactful" since every option must be exhaustively audited against a shared budget ([EN World forum synthesis](https://www.enworld.org/threads/regarding-the-complexity-of-pathfinder-2.675063/)) — a cost this project can likely absorb, since audited-budget discipline is something an AI-adjudicated system needs regardless.

**Action economy design** shows a clean three-way split in how systems prevent "more turns worth of stuff" from being the dominant strategy, and the failure modes are as instructive as the successes. D&D 5e's fixed action/bonus-action/reaction split is simple to learn but creates a documented secondary optimization metagame: "some players optimize their character build to have a Bonus Action to use every round, which makes their turns feel more fulfilling," producing a "turn fulfillment" disparity between bonus-action-rich classes (Rogues, Monks, bonus-action spellcasters) and single-action classes independent of raw power ([DungeonHeaven](https://www.dungeonheaven.com/post/action-economy-in-dungeons-dragons-5e)) — and separately, the system has no coherent answer for summoned-ally or Haste-stacked action multiplication, with the DMG itself not "specify[ing] how this factors into encounter balancing math" ([Board N Byte](https://www.boardnbyte.com/post/balancing-the-battlefield-mastering-action-economy-in-d-d-5e-encounters)). Pathfinder 2e's fix is structural rather than curative: every character gets the *same* flat pool of three actions plus one reaction regardless of build, so tactical diversity is pushed entirely into *what fills the actions* rather than *how many actions a build has*, and diminishing-return penalties on repeated Strikes (−5 on the second attack, −10 on the third) explicitly discourage "just attack three times" as a dominant strategy ([Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/pathfinder-2e-action-economy-guide-beginner/)). 4th-edition D&D took a third approach, tying action availability to the encounter/daily cycle (at-will/encounter/daily powers plus milestone-earned Action Points) rather than turn-by-turn structure, which controls economy at the fight level rather than the turn level ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/the-economy-of-actions-in-4e.235001/)). Lancer supplies the sharpest single insight of this whole deep-dive: rather than trying to make PCs and NPCs symmetric, it deliberately builds asymmetric action budgets — NPCs cannot Brace (a defensive reaction) or Overcharge (extra actions at a heat cost) the way PCs can — accepting the asymmetry as a feature that "encourage[s] tactical play around those differences" rather than a bug to eliminate ([Kazkar.ai](https://kazkar.ai/blog/lancer-vs-dnd-combat)). For this project, PF2e's fixed-pool-with-variable-costs is the clear front-runner (it closes off the action-stacking exploit class entirely and generalizes cleanly to an AI-computable rule), and Lancer's asymmetric-NPC-budget trick is a genuinely new, directly reusable idea for anything the AI GM needs to run as an opponent rather than a player-controlled entity.

**Audited/point-buy cost systems** are this pass's clearest warning case, and the evidence against a naive shared point pool is now stacked from four independent directions. GURPS (from the prior report) already showed that a flat point pool "creates identity, not balance," where a 200-point specialist "can be chopped into kindling" by a narrowly-optimized 40-point fighter, precisely because a flat bonus to a broad attribute is cheaper than raising narrow skills individually, and disadvantages are gameable by taking ones with "little or no tangible effect on play." This pass adds three more data points that converge on the same lesson from different systems. Mutants & Masterminds explicitly does *not* try to prevent power disparity — it contains it instead, via a hard Power Level ceiling on combat-relevant stats (attack, defense, toughness), openly trading exploit-resistance for flexibility and delegating the actual balance decision to the GM's choice of campaign Power Level ([TV Tropes](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/TabletopGame/MutantsAndMasterminds); [Reality Refracted](http://www.realityrefracted.com/2011/08/power-level-limits.html?m=1)). The HERO System is the most exploit-resistant of the point-buy family surveyed, and its method is worth copying directly: discrete, non-continuous cost brackets by power type (Dispel at 3 points/die, Drain at 6, Killing Attacks at 15) prevent linear arbitrage between similar effects, defense is deliberately cheaper than offense as a built-in counterweight, and players are forced to spend points across skills, disadvantages, and powers simultaneously rather than dumping everything into one axis ([PDB Wiki](https://pdb.fandom.com/wiki/Hero_System)). Savage Worlds solves the "disadvantages as free points" exploit with forced equilibrium — Hindrances grant points, Edges cost points, and the character sheet must balance to exactly zero, with hard caps of one Major and two Minor Hindrances to stop disadvantage-stacking ([RPG Writeups](https://writeups.letsyouandhimfight.com/jackmann/savage-worlds/)). 13th Age sidesteps the entire problem category by not using a shared numeric currency at all for its distinctiveness mechanic — One Unique Thing and Icon Relationships are narratively rather than mathematically balanced, so there is no point-pool to arbitrage ([Pelgrane Press](https://pelgranepress.com/2022/05/14/one-shot-unique-things/)). The cross-system pattern is now unambiguous: a shared point currency is safe only when *every single option's cost has been independently audited against every other option*, which is exactly the discipline PF2e brings to its action economy and exactly what 5e's 2024 revision failed to do across its own martial/caster ledger — for an AI GM with no equivalent of a human GM's live policing ("an eagle-eyed GM" is explicitly named as the GURPS-era stopgap), this makes a naive shared point-buy the single riskiest of the four mechanics to adopt without HERO-style tiered, forced-distribution guardrails built in from day one.

**Move/verb-based ability design** is the one mechanic in this deep-dive that trades raw option-space for near-zero adjudication ambiguity, and the research surfaces a genuine design spectrum rather than a single answer. At the fully-pre-specified end, Blades in the Dark gives each playbook exactly seven narrowly-triggered special abilities (versus PbtA's typical 10–12 moves), producing "cleaner narrative resolution" with fewer "does this move apply?" disputes, at the cost of abilities that "feel less impactful than increasing base Action ratings" because they are mostly incremental (+1d, +1 effect) rather than transformative ([EN World](https://enworld.org/threads/blades-in-the-dark-advice-please.702150/page-6)). At the template-guided middle, Fate Core's stunts are built from exactly three named patterns — add a new action, grant a situational bonus, or carve a narrow rules exception — each explicitly constrained by "narrow circumstances" (specific actions only, situational restrictions, frequency caps like "once per scene") and chained into prerequisite "stunt families" that let power grow by branching into new flavors rather than by stacking raw numbers ([Fate Core SRD](https://fate-srd.com/fate-core/building-stunts)). This is a genuinely new, highly transferable idea beyond what the prior two reports found: a designer defines a small closed set of *ability-authoring templates* (not a fixed list of abilities, but a fixed list of *ways abilities are allowed to be shaped*), and players or a generative system can then author within those templates without breaking the game's power budget, because "narrow circumstances" and "frequency caps" are load-bearing, non-optional parts of every template rather than case-by-case tuning. The open-framework end of the spectrum is deliberately absent from any surveyed system's actual ruleset — every real example this pass found stays either fully pre-specified or template-constrained — which is itself informative: no serious published TTRPG trusts fully open player-authored abilities without either a fixed list or a fixed template grammar, reinforcing rather than contradicting this project's existing bias toward closed combinatorial primitives.

## The tools landscape confirms the architecture and exposes the fairness gap

Non-AI branching-narrative tools matter to this project less as competitors than as proof that state management and narrative flexibility are not actually in tension when the tooling is designed well. Ink treats state as first-class from the ground up — typed variables, serializable-to-JSON story state, and parallel simultaneous story threads that can affect each other — while remaining "entirely script-driven... designed for writers," not programmers ([Inkle](https://www.inklestudios.com/ink/)). Yarn Spinner makes the same tradeoff even more approachably, with a Dialogue Runner bridging plain-language scripts to a Variable Storage component that persists across sessions, explicitly aimed at "writers without programming experience" ([Yarn Spinner](https://yarnspinner.dev/)). Twine shows the opposite end of the accessibility-versus-scale tradeoff: its visual node editor has the lowest barrier to entry of any tool surveyed, but "visual clutter... can become hard to navigate" once branching graphs get dense, and its pluggable story-format architecture (Harlowe vs. SugarCube) means state-management power varies by which engine a given project chose. ChoiceScript is the most directly relevant precedent for a stat-driven LitRPG-style project specifically because it was built narrowly for "stat-based interactive novels" with `*create`/`*set`/`*if` primitives as first-class citizens of the language rather than bolted-on macros — proof that designing explicitly for one narrative pattern (accumulated-stat branching) produces cleaner tooling than a general-purpose branching engine retrofitted for stats. None of these tools generate content with AI, but the transferable lesson — cited explicitly in the sourced synthesis of this research — is that "even if your game is AI-driven," their approach to typed variables, serialized state, and node/jump structuring "transfer" directly as patterns for how an AI GM's external state store should be shaped.

The AI-driven roleplay platform landscape beyond AI Dungeon converges on one specific architectural fix, independently reinvented by nearly every serious player: an external, structured knowledge layer that survives what the raw context window cannot hold. NovelAI's Lorebook is the cleanest example — persistent, retrievable world and character knowledge injected alongside generation, which the sourced synthesis credits as demonstrating that "persistent external knowledge... significantly improves narrative coherence compared to context-window-only approaches," even though NovelAI still concedes that "free-form generation can drift off-track after extended play." SillyTavern generalizes the same idea into a full retrieval-augmented character-and-lorebook system with tag-based dynamic insertion, while remaining explicitly model-agnostic and local-first — its popularity is direct evidence that a meaningful user segment values raw control over convenience, "the IDE for writers metaphor" as the sourced framing puts it. KoboldAI's Adventure Mode is the most game-like of the surveyed platforms but is also candidly documented as still having "problematic" state management with "drifting narratives" despite a real engineering investment in state broadcasting (a `koboldai_vars` single-source-of-truth class with attribute-interception change propagation). Character.AI's documented decline is a useful negative data point for this project's tone: heavy safety guardrails plus memory resets after "dozens of messages" broke narrative continuity badly enough to motivate an entire wave of less-restricted competitors — corroborating the prior report's AI Dungeon finding that safety/trust failures and narrative-grounding failures are genuinely separate problems needing separate fixes, now confirmed by a second, independent commercial-platform case study.

Open-source AI-DM projects supply the field's most direct engineering precedent for this project's own architecture question, and they split cleanly along the same axis the prior report already identified — narrative-driven state versus engine-owned state — but this pass finds a named middle pattern worth adopting. NarrativeEngine's two-tier memory design is the most sophisticated: persistent character/location "ledgers" stay separate from a dynamic RAG layer for factions, economy, and events (roughly 1,200 tokens/turn), explicitly because "this dual approach avoids polluting the context window with static data" — a practical, load-tested version of the "externalized structured store" this project's own prior-report architecture already called for, and the closest thing to a working reference implementation found in either research pass. Open Dungeon sits at the opposite, more conservative pole: narration is fully LLM-generated, but game state is "100% deterministic and engine-owned," guaranteeing "no narrative can break rules" at the direct cost that "narration is constrained to describe game state, limiting creative freedom" — the sharpest available real-world statement of the tradeoff this project will have to resolve for itself. Both projects independently confirm, from the builder's-eye view rather than the academic literature's, that the fundamental tension is real and that every serious attempt resolves it by drawing the state-authority line somewhere rather than trusting the LLM with both narration and truth simultaneously.

The academic literature adds three genuinely new layers beyond what the second report already found on function-calling and judge-gating. First, evaluation methodology has matured well past simple consistency scoring: RPGBench is now a full benchmark for LLMs as RPG engines, splitting evaluation into objective mechanical verification and subjective LLM-judged content quality ([arXiv:2502.00595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00595)); NARRA-Gym supplies an 11-dimension rubric (7 story-quality + 4 user-experience dimensions) scored by a three-model LLM-judge sweep specifically to reduce single-judge bias, plus human ranking for qualities "difficult to reduce to rubric anchors" like whether "emotional turns feel earned rather than merely correct" ([arXiv:2605.08503](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08503)); and a validated 23-dimension Story Quality Dimension taxonomy, built via Delphi study with 10 narrative-design experts, found 78% of dimensions scored 3.5+ on importance and identified two emergent, previously-uncatalogued dimensions (distinctive narrative Voice and Genre Alignment) ([arXiv:2509.04239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04239)) — together these give this project an off-the-shelf menu of evaluation axes rather than needing to invent its own from scratch. Second, and more sobering, long-horizon consistency research quantifies exactly how far the state-desync problem the prior report flagged (46.1% exact-match state prediction) actually extends: NCP-Bench finds that even GPT-5.2, the strongest model tested, survives only **42% of environments to 20 turns without a logical conflict**, with fact conflicts as the dominant failure mode (40–68% of failures across models), and explicitly states that "only isolated runs satisfied all achievement commitments within the 100-turn limit" even though human baseline testing confirms the underlying task is solvable ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)) — this is materially worse than the prior report's already-sobering 46.1% figure and should reset this project's expectations downward again: no currently deployed LLM, agentic or otherwise, reliably holds a consistent world state past a couple dozen turns without explicit external enforcement. A companion taxonomy usefully decomposes *why*: five error categories (timeline/plot logic, characterization, world-building, factual/detail, narrative style), with factual and temporal errors dominant and clustering "around the middle of narratives" ([arXiv:2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05890)) — meaning entity-tracking and temporal reasoning specifically, not general narrative quality, are where an externalized-state layer needs to concentrate its enforcement effort.

Third, and most important for this project's open architecture question: **fairness in adjudicating a player's novel, rules-unanticipated action remains entirely unaddressed in the published literature.** This pass searched specifically for it and found nothing — the closest adjacent work is adversarial-robustness research on LLM judges (an attacker-defender co-evolving game for safety alignment, and benchmarks for judge robustness under distribution shift) ([arXiv:2602.01539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01539); [aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.199](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.199.pdf)) and one directly relevant psychological finding: player acceptance of AI adjudication depends on *perceived locus of control* — "the same AI capability was acceptable when it expanded valued play experiences, but problematic when it appeared to take over judgment, authorship, or player choice" ([arXiv:2502.19519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19519)). That finding reframes the fairness problem usefully: the design target is not "the AI's rulings are statistically correct" but "the AI's rulings visibly preserve the player's sense that their choice mattered," which is a UX/framing requirement layered on top of, not a substitute for, mechanical consistency. Multi-agent GM architectures are the other genuinely new thread, and they converge on a pattern this project should take seriously as a concrete implementation option: a fabula/discourse split, where a Director/simulation agent plans world-state events and a separate Narrator agent translates only the structured events relevant to the current scene into prose, "feeding it only the context needed for that specific scene" ([wordplay-workshop.github.io](https://wordplay-workshop.github.io/pdfs/20.pdf)) — this is a direct architectural instantiation of the "externalized state, narration is downstream of it" principle the prior report inferred more abstractly. PLAYER*'s three mechanisms (sensor-driven question generation, action-space pruning, and anytime search-tree planning borrowed from robotics) demonstrate that adaptive, information-driven agent planning generalizes better across scenarios than static, hand-authored guides ([arXiv:2404.17662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17662)), and RuleSmith's self-play balancing — two LLM agents independently controlling opposing factions, with a Bayesian-optimization loop tuning a 12-dimensional rule space to minimize win-rate disparity ([arXiv:2602.06232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06232)) — is a genuinely novel, automatable technique this project could borrow directly for balancing its own closed combinatorial primitive space without waiting for enough live playtests to surface every exploit manually.

## What this round changes about the prior conclusions

Nothing this pass found contradicts the two prior reports' three central claims, but each claim now has sharper edges and, in one case, a genuinely new corollary. **Small closed combinatorial primitives** gains its strongest normative argument yet: the audited-cost deep-dive shows that *any* shared numeric currency without exhaustive per-option auditing collapses the same way GURPS, D&D 5e's feats, and D&D 5e's 2024 caster/martial ledger all collapsed, while PF2e's discipline of auditing the *entire* option space against a shared budget — not just the newly-changed half — is the one documented example of the discipline actually working. This converts "small closed primitives" from a build-diversity heuristic into a governance requirement: whatever primitive set this project settles on, it needs a standing audit process, not a one-time balance pass, or it will independently discover the same 5e-2024 failure regardless of how disciplined the initial design was. **Additive stacking without retirement** gains three new confirmed instances from entirely different domains than the LitRPG novels and Mistborn material already covered — D&D 5e's feat/supplement bloat, 13th Age's Icon Connection rules-text bloat (3 pages to 60), and the general indie-design "option bloat" antipattern — meaning this failure mode is not specific to progression-fantasy fiction or to character sheets; it is a property of *any* long-running system that adds content without a symmetric subtraction mechanism, rules text included. The **three-layer AI-GM architecture** gains both a harder number (42% 20-turn survival even on the strongest tested model, worse than the 46.1% figure the second report already flagged) and its first credible reference implementation pattern (NarrativeEngine's ledger-plus-RAG split, and the fabula/discourse multi-agent split), which together argue this project should budget for the reconciliation/verification layer as a first-class, expensive-to-build component rather than an afterthought bolted onto a single narrating model.

The genuinely new addition this pass contributes, though, is that **spotlight balance and challenge diversification must be engineered as explicit, standing subsystems** — a per-player, per-campaign-arc spotlight ledger and a challenge-type rotation policy — because no source anywhere, in either academic AI-GM literature or GMing craft literature, shows a system that gets these for free from good mechanical design alone. And the fairness-in-novel-action-adjudication gap, confirmed as still completely open by a second, more targeted search in this pass, should be named explicitly in the project constitution as a known unsolved problem rather than something the judge-gate architecture is assumed to handle by extension — the psychological finding on locus-of-control suggests the practical fix may need to be as much about how a ruling is *framed and explained* to the player as about how it is computed.

## Updated primitive list and verdicts on the four deep-dived mechanics

The table below carries forward every row from the two prior reports' lists, adds this round's new material in place where it sharpens an existing row, and appends new rows at the bottom grouped by what they govern. Rows unchanged from the second report are omitted here for space; consult that report for the full carried-forward list of build-diversity, resource-cost, and architecture primitives (fixed slot×pool combinatorics, hidden variance layers, narrative-truth-gated tiers, debt/loan resource economies, the three-layer architecture, judge-gate independence, etc.) — none of them are contradicted by this pass, and several are reinforced as noted above.

| Primitive / pattern | Source(s) this round | What it buys you | Tradeoff / cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit spotlight-balance ledger** (per-player, tracked across the campaign arc, not the session) | Alexandrian, Gooey Cube, Robin's Laws player archetypes | Gives an AI GM the one thing it structurally lacks — a human GM's felt sense that "X hasn't shone in a while" — as a queryable, auditable state | No off-the-shelf algorithm exists; must be designed from scratch, and a naive session-level metric will misfire since balancing unit is the arc, not the session |
| **Challenge-type rotation policy** (environmental / tactical / objective-based / resource-pressure / encounter-mixing, cycled deliberately rather than escalating stats) | Alexandrian's Optimization Arms Race, Sly Flourish, Scroll for Initiative | Concrete, named encounter-generation levers that let an optimized build feel challenged without raw stat inflation | Requires a content-generation layer diverse enough to actually produce all five challenge types, not just combat |
| **Standing option-audit process (not a one-time balance pass)** | D&D 5e's 2024 revision failure, PF2e's whole-ledger discipline | Prevents the single most common real-world failure — fixing one side of an asymmetry while the other side keeps moving | High ongoing design/maintenance cost; must be budgeted as a permanent process, not a launch milestone |
| **Passive abilities must be structurally weak; power should live in active/conditional triggers** | 13th Age's Ranger fix (1e→2e) | A concrete, falsifiable design rule for where to put power in a build, rather than case-by-case tuning | Reduces the space for "always-on" flavor abilities; some archetypes (turtling, buffing) are inherently passive and will feel underpowered under this rule unless specifically exempted |
| **Ability-authoring templates (fixed set of *shapes* abilities may take, not a fixed list of abilities)** | Fate Core's three stunt patterns (new action / situational bonus / rules exception), each with mandatory narrow-circumstance and frequency-cap constraints | Lets a generative system (or players) author new abilities within guardrails, without an ever-growing hand-authored list and without breaking the power budget | Requires the template grammar to be genuinely comprehensive (three patterns must cover nearly everything wanted) or players will constantly request off-pattern abilities |
| **Asymmetric NPC action budgets** (deliberately different rules for what NPCs vs. PCs can do on a turn) | Lancer (NPCs can't Brace/Overcharge) | A clean way to keep NPC-side computation and balance tractable without needing PC-NPC symmetry | Players may perceive it as unfair if the asymmetry isn't narratively motivated or transparently communicated |
| **Self-play balance testing** (two agents independently control opposing sides, optimize a rule-space parameter to minimize win-rate disparity) | RuleSmith | An automatable alternative to manual playtesting for surfacing exploits in a closed combinatorial primitive space | Requires a working simulation/self-play harness; only as good as the fidelity of the simulated agents' strategies |
| **Fabula/discourse multi-agent split** (a Director/simulation agent owns world-state events; a separate Narrator agent turns only the scene-relevant events into prose) | TRPG multi-agent GM research | A concrete, implementable version of "externalized state, narration downstream of it" | Adds inter-agent coordination complexity; no published research yet on how to resolve disagreements between the two agents |
| **Off-the-shelf evaluation rubric** (23 validated Story Quality Dimensions; 11-dimension NARRA-Gym rubric; multi-model judge sweep to reduce single-judge bias) | RPGBench, NARRA-Gym, SQD Delphi study | Avoids re-inventing evaluation criteria from scratch; gives concrete axes to test against before and after any mechanic change | Rubrics are validated for general interactive-narrative quality, not specifically for TTRPG rules-adjudication fairness, which remains unaddressed |
| **Named, explicit unsolved-problem flag: fairness in novel-action adjudication** | Second targeted search, still empty | Prevents the project constitution from silently assuming the judge-gate architecture solves this by extension | No mitigation exists yet beyond the locus-of-control framing insight (rulings should visibly preserve the sense that player choice mattered) |

Weighing the four deep-dived mechanics directly against each other and against this project's stated constraints (LitRPG-flavored build diversity, live AI adjudication, no human GM in the loop): **degrees-of-success resolution is the strongest fit of the four** and should move from candidate to near-default, because PF2e's threshold-margin rule is fully computable with no judgment call, generalizes cleanly to "did the improvised action work" (this project's single hardest recurring adjudication question), and its only real cost — reduced per-bonus impact under exhaustive auditing — is a cost this project needs to pay for other reasons anyway. **A fixed, build-independent action-economy pool with variable per-action costs is the second-strongest fit**, again because it is PF2e's approach: it closes off the entire class of action-stacking exploits (summons, Haste-chaining) that plague D&D 5e specifically because the DMG never audited them, and Lancer's asymmetric-NPC-budget trick is a directly reusable bonus for keeping the AI's opponent-side computation simple. **Move/verb-based ability design is a good but partial fit**: it is the right tool specifically for flavor/narrative abilities that shouldn't get full numeric modeling, and Fate's template-pattern approach (rather than Blades' or PbtA's fully-fixed lists) is the specific version to borrow, since it supports open-ended player/generative authorship inside hard guardrails — but it is not a substitute for a full build-diversity engine on its own, since its total option space per "playbook" stays intentionally small. **Audited/point-buy cost systems are the weakest fit of the four as commonly implemented**, and this pass's evidence hardens rather than softens that verdict from the prior report: GURPS, M&M's containment-not-prevention approach, and even D&D 5e's own feat economy all show that a shared point currency fails without either (a) HERO-style discrete cost brackets plus forced cross-category spending, or (b) Savage Worlds-style forced zero-sum equilibrium — meaning this project should either adopt one of those two specific guardrail patterns wholesale if it wants any shared-currency customization at all, or avoid a shared point pool entirely in favor of the closed-slot combinatorics both prior reports already favored.
