# Skip the playthrough, keep the rigor

Building a long-horizon consistency stress test out of a synthetic "story so far" recap plus pre-seeded state, then running real turns on top, is not a shortcut that compromises rigor — it is the *dominant* methodology in the 2025-2026 literature, not a departure from it. NCP-Bench, the most directly relevant benchmark, builds every one of its 100 test environments this exact way: a three-stage LLM extraction pipeline (trajectory → commitments → initial facts) manufactures a structured world-state skeleton from a movie synopsis, and only the *live* interaction turns are generated fresh, by a scripted player persona, never a human playtester ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). RPGBench does the same thing one level up: its "games" are entirely LLM-authored JSON worlds, mechanically validated by a reachability checker before any simulation begins ([arXiv:2502.00595](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00595)). The literature's actual concern is not "synthetic vs. real" — it is whether the synthetic construction avoids leaking future knowledge into the seeded state and whether the categories of seeded facts match where real models actually break. On both counts, the evidence is specific and actionable: fact contradictions (not plot-structure or player-responsiveness failures) dominate every benchmark's error budget, degradation is severe well before any planned session length is reached (the best model in NCP-Bench survives only 42% of runs to turn 20), and the one architectural mitigation actually tested — hierarchical memory — traded a large reduction in commitment violations for a near-tripling of player-input-ignoring failures, an unresolved tradeoff the source paper states outright. For Feature 002, this means the proposed methodology is sound in principle, but its value depends entirely on faithfully reproducing NCP-Bench's isolation discipline during recap construction and on deliberately seeding the fact categories these benchmarks show fail first.

## NCP-Bench proves synthetic construction is the field's actual standard, not a compromise

NCP-Bench formalizes long-horizon narrative consistency as a constraint-satisfaction problem over an explicit state `st = (Ft, it, C, ℛ)` — a fact ledger, a trajectory index, a set of typed commitments, and an ordered reference trajectory — and, critically, **builds every one of its 100 test environments without playing a single real turn** ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). The construction pipeline is a `SpecificationGenerator` running three sequential, dependent LLM calls, each with a dedicated, publicly available prompt template: **trajectory extraction** (`specification_trajectory.txt`) turns a movie synopsis into an ordered array of atomic plot nodes, each with a `trigger_event` and a `key_delta` defined as "a factual, observable, or otherwise judgeable change at the world-state level" — explicitly not a mental-state-only change — and enforces a **point-of-view constraint** forbidding omniscient narration that the player-role character couldn't plausibly know ([GitHub raw](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench/main/src/ncpbench/prompts/specification_trajectory.txt)). **Commitment extraction** (`specification_commitments.txt`) is then conditioned on that trajectory and emits invariant/ordering/achievement commitments, each with an explicit `satisfaction_condition` and `violation_condition`, required to be "fact-based, mutually exclusive, and unique" and "directly determinable" without interpretive judgment — static background facts like names or occupations are explicitly excluded ([GitHub raw](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench/main/src/ncpbench/prompts/specification_commitments.txt)). **Initial-fact extraction** (`specification_initial_facts.txt`) is conditioned on both prior stages and governed by what the prompt itself names the **"T=0 Absolute Isolation Principle"**: facts describe only the cross-sectional world state at the story's starting node, with zero "logical leakage" of future plot knowledge — the generator is explicitly told to positively assert ignorance ("someone is not yet aware," "something has not yet happened") rather than simply omit unknown information ([GitHub raw](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench/main/src/ncpbench/prompts/specification_initial_facts.txt)). Only *after* this static skeleton exists does NCP-Bench generate the actual multi-turn history — live, at evaluation time, via a separate constrained player-persona LLM (`adversarial_input.txt` casts a suspicious, exploit-seeking player; `natural_input.txt` casts a cooperative one, both capped at 50 words and restricted to visible history only) — with a `fact_update.txt` prompt reconciling the fact ledger after every turn ([GitHub raw](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench/main/src/ncpbench/prompts/fact_update.txt)). The entire pipeline is MIT-licensed and published as runnable code, not just described in the paper — `SpecificationGenerator(client).generate(source)` is designed to be re-pointed at arbitrary source material via a pluggable LLM client ([GitHub README](https://github.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench)). The direct implication for Feature 002 is that a synthetic recap is not a weaker substitute for "real" turns — it is the same three-stage extraction discipline NCP-Bench itself uses, and the specific mechanism that makes it trustworthy (rather than hand-waved) is the T=0 isolation principle and the requirement that commitments be anchored to the trajectory that was extracted immediately before them, not invented independently.

## RPGBench and the wider field confirm the "real turns on top" half of the design

If NCP-Bench validates the synthetic-skeleton half of the proposed methodology, RPGBench validates the second half — that a stress test still needs a genuine forward-running simulation to measure drift, rather than treating the synthetic scenario as the whole test. RPGBench's Game Simulation task requires the model to run a pre-validated, LLM-authored JSON world for a fixed number of rounds (10 in the main experiment, extended to 15/20/25 in an ablation), narrating outcomes and updating state each round; a harness then mechanically replays the game's own declared rules against the model's prior state and diffs the recomputed "should-be" state against what the model actually emitted, producing a mechanics score (MEC) that requires zero LLM judgment ([arXiv:2502.00595](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00595)). That length ablation is the paper's clearest finding for a long-horizon test design: MEC reliably **declines as trajectory length increases**, even though every model is required to emit the same enforced structured JSON state block every round — meaning enforced structure alone does not prevent degradation; models still make outright arithmetic and logical errors constructing that structure as rounds accumulate ([arXiv:2502.00595](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00595)). Crucially, RPGBench's model-generated worlds pass through a **BFS validity checker** before ever being used — confirming that reachability of both success and failure states, and reachability of every declared event, is a released, code-level construction requirement, not an incidental nicety ([GitHub](https://github.com/boson-ai/rpgbench-public)). The broader agent-memory literature converges on the same two-part structure. "The Horizon Gap" survey, reviewing 1,547 papers from 2024-2026, explicitly separates **long-context** (token capacity), **long-horizon** (required steps), and **long-term memory** (persistence across steps) as distinct properties that get conflated ([arXiv:2608.06663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06663)) — Feature 002's stress test is squarely testing the second and third, and a synthetic recap is precisely how you manufacture the "long-horizon" condition (accumulated prior steps) without needing to also manufacture 80 turns of real generation just to get there. The one caveat this comparison surfaces: RPGBench never tests a model tracking state in pure freeform prose against a structured-JSON condition head-to-head — that ablation doesn't exist anywhere in the literature searched, meaning Feature 002's own tool-calling architecture (externalized JSON state via real `get_state`/`update_state` calls, rather than narrative-embedded JSON parsed post-hoc as RPGBench does) is testing genuinely unexplored territory, not replicating a known result.

## Fact conflicts, not plot or player-responsiveness failures, are where models actually break

Across every source in this research pass, the finding repeats with different vocabulary: models fail at long-horizon consistency primarily by **contradicting previously established world state**, not by mishandling plot sequencing or ignoring the player. NCP-Bench's own conflict taxonomy — fact conflicts, commitment conflicts (ordering/invariant/achievement), and player-input conflicts — shows fact conflicts dominating at **40-68% of all conflicts across six tested models** (GPT-5.2, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3.2, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Kimi-K2.5, Grok-4.1-Fast), consistently ahead of commitment or player-input conflict rates ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)). Its four qualitative failure categories, ranked roughly by prevalence, are **Hallucination & Factual Contradiction** (asserting something that contradicts the fact ledger — the dominant mode), **Triggering Unknown Facts** (prematurely disclosing information the trajectory says shouldn't yet be known), **Forcibly Changing Reality** (silently erasing a previously completed player action), and **Ignoring User Input** ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). Its appendix case studies are concretely instructive for what to seed: spatial/location contradiction (a character's location silently changes with no narrated movement, in an *Alien*-based environment), object-state contradiction (a "locked door" fact later treated as freely accessible with no unlocking event narrated, in an *Indiana Jones* environment), character-status contradiction (alive/dead or present/absent status silently violated, in a *Zombieland* environment), and entity-existence contradiction (a character or object referenced as pre-established when it was actually introduced later, in a *Casino Royale* environment) ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). "Lost in Stories" (ConStory-Bench) supplies an independent, complementary taxonomy — 5 categories, 19 subtypes — built from actual long-form generation rather than interactive dialogue, and its empirical ranking is genuinely informative: although **Characterization** (Memory Contradictions, Knowledge Contradictions, Skill Fluctuations, Forgotten Abilities) and **World-building & Setting** (Core Rules Violations, Social Norms Violations, Geographical Contradictions) are the categories conceptually closest to "the AI forgets what it already established," the paper's own data shows **Factual & Detail Consistency** (Appearance Mismatches, Nomenclature Confusions, Quantitative Mismatches) and **Timeline & Plot Logic** (Absolute/Duration/Simultaneity Contradictions, Causeless Effects, Causal Logic Violations, Abandoned Plot Elements) are the *empirically dominant* failure modes, not just the conceptually obvious ones ([arXiv:2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)). That paper also reports that the average distance between a planted fact and its eventual contradiction varies sharply by category — geographical contradictions have the largest gap (31.0% of story length) while perspective confusions have the smallest (4.7%) — meaning some error types are genuine long-range forgetting while others are near-term slips that ordinary short-context attention should already catch ([arXiv:2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)). The general agent-memory literature adds one more sharply quantified pattern with direct relevance to a D&D-style state system: **facts that *change over time* are categorically the most fragile fact type**, not any particular narrative content category. LOCOMO-style benchmarks consistently show temporal reasoning as the weakest category relative to single-hop lookup ([EmergentMind](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/locomo-benchmark-d06cff1a-d4a5-4df8-ab85-fdca157d190b)), and the MERIT/FAMA cost-aware evaluation found embedding-retrieval accuracy on *updated* facts collapses unpredictably into a 0.30-0.95 range (a 0.45 max-seed gap), versus a much tighter 0.70-1.00 band for update-on-write structured stores ([arXiv:2609.05441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)). For a D&D engine, this maps directly onto quantitative state (HP, inventory counts, gold, position) and status flags (locked/unlocked, alive/dead, known/unknown to a given NPC) that get *revised* mid-session — exactly the categories a naive test would under-seed in favor of static color (names, backstory, room descriptions).

## Degradation is fast, severe, and largely decoupled from raw turn survival

The quantified thresholds across sources should recalibrate what "success" means for an 80-100 turn test. NCP-Bench's headline result: even its best-performing narrator, GPT-5.2, achieves only a **42% survival rate after just 20 turns**, with fact-conflict rates ranging **40-68%** across the six models tested, and only isolated runs satisfying all achievement commitments within the full 100-turn limit ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08160)). Across all models and both player conditions combined, only about **3.5% of interactions reached the 100-turn cap without a single flagged conflict** ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). Per-model average survival before termination ranged from Kimi-K2.5's 2.88 turns up to GPT-5.2's 32.92 turns under adversarial play, with weaker models like Grok-4.1-Fast averaging under 8 turns ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)) — meaning a fixed 80-100 turn target session length will, for most current models, simply never be reached intact; the test needs to measure *where* and *how* failure happens, not only whether the full length was completed. A cooperative ("natural") player roughly doubles survival length versus an adversarial one (46.08 vs. 22.16 average turns for GPT-4o-mini) and cuts commitment-conflict rate by roughly 3x, but barely moves the fact-conflict rate (56% vs. 60%) — implying fact-tracking failure is driven mostly by accumulated turn count itself, not adversarial pressure, and an adversarial player condition mainly accelerates *when* the same underlying failure surfaces ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). RPGBench's complementary finding is that mechanical consistency (MEC) measurably **declines with round count even under fully enforced structured JSON output** in its {10, 15, 20, 25}-round ablation on GPT-4o, alongside a companion decline in narrative interestingness attributed to repetition ([arXiv:2502.00595](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00595)) — direct evidence that Feature 001's tool-calling architecture, which already forces state through real `roll_check`/`get_state`/`update_state` calls rather than narrative-embedded JSON, will not be immune to this trend just because the schema is enforced; enforcement changes *how* errors surface (a malformed tool call vs. a silent prose contradiction) but not whether they occur. "Lost in Stories" quantifies error density directly: Consistency Error Density (errors per 10K words) ranges from GPT-5-Reasoning's best-in-class 0.113 up to 0.537 for Qwen3-32B, with errors accumulating roughly **linearly with output length** and established facts typically planted early-to-mid (15-30% normalized position) while contradictions cluster later (40-60% normalized position) ([arXiv:2603.05890](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.05890v1)). Finally, the MERIT framework's finding that agents act correctly on a *correctly retrieved* updated fact only **55% of the time** ([arXiv:2609.05441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)) is a sobering ceiling: even if Feature 002's state layer perfectly returns the right value from `get_state`, the narration layer has a roughly coin-flip chance of actually using it correctly in prose — which is precisely the narration-vs-state divergence Feature 001 was built to detect, now shown to be a known, quantified, and unsolved failure mode rather than a hypothetical risk.

## Mitigation architecture: adopt the cheap layers now, treat hierarchical memory as an open research question

Only one mitigation architecture has been directly benchmarked against this exact failure mode, and its result is a genuine, stated tradeoff rather than a clean win. NCP-Bench compared a plain GPT-4o-mini baseline against **HiAgent**, a hierarchical/summarization-style memory-augmented narrator: HiAgent extended average survival from 22.16 to 30.05 turns and cut commitment conflicts sharply (26%→4%), but **player-input conflicts more than doubled (13%→38%)** — the paper attributes this to memory-compression losses and states outright that "improving long-horizon commitment preservation without degrading local input fidelity remains an open challenge" ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). Notably, trajectory progress and satisfied-commitment percentage both *slightly decreased* under HiAgent despite the longer survival (16.64%→15.68% and 10.03%→9.14% respectively) — surviving longer did not translate into more actual story progress, meaning "not crashing" and "advancing the story correctly" are separable failure surfaces that should be measured independently ([arXiv:2608.08160](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08160)). The wider memory literature explains *why* this tradeoff appears and what to do instead. Practitioner consensus on summarization drift holds that "each compression pass silently discards low-frequency details, and after enough passes, the agent 'remembers' a sanitized, generic version of history that fails on edge cases," and recommends preserving **verbatim** the most recent ~10% of context (current tool calls, most recent turns) while only compressing older, already-acted-upon material — and avoiding compounding a second summarization pass over an already-summarized history ([Morph](https://www.morphllm.com/compaction-vs-summarization)). BOOKMARKS operationalizes this alternative directly for role-play: instead of compressing history, it keeps the full storyline accessible and retrieves task-relevant content on demand via lightweight "bookmark" checkpoints, improving mystery-game reasoning accuracy from 39.44% to 45.42% over an incremental-summarization baseline, with lower compute overhead than full re-summarization ([arXiv:2605.14169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14169)). The single largest quantified consistency win found anywhere in this research is not a memory architecture at all but a **post-hoc verification/audit step**: a structured-knowledge-tree detective-game system that checks generated dialogue against a knowledge store before it reaches the player cut critical hallucinations by **64.78%** and entirely prevented premature information disclosure, at some cost to conversational naturalness ([arXiv:2609.23043](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23043)) — this is architecturally the same move as NCP-Bench's own auditor (a second, narrowly-scoped LLM call that checks narration against an explicit fact/commitment ledger) and the same move Feature 001 already validated at small scale via its independent LLM-judge check. Two structural recommendations follow with reasonable confidence: keep the *most recent* raw turns fully un-compressed while summarizing only older material rather than summarizing uniformly, and prefer update-on-write structured stores over embedding-based retrieval for anything that changes — the 0.70-1.00 vs. 0.30-0.95 stability gap on updated facts is the sharpest quantified signal in this literature ([arXiv:2609.05441](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05441)). Full hierarchical summarization (HiAgent-style) should be treated as a later-phase experiment, not a default for Feature 002, precisely because its own source paper frames the player-input-fidelity cost as unresolved.

## What this means for Feature 002's spec

The synthesis points to five concrete decisions. First, **construct the recap with NCP-Bench's three-stage pipeline, not as freehand prose**: write a short campaign/session outline (functionally the "synopsis"), then run three sequential LLM extraction calls — trajectory nodes (atomic, each with a causally-connected `trigger_event` and an observable `key_delta`, respecting POV so nothing the player-character couldn't know leaks in), commitments conditioned on that trajectory (typed invariant/ordering/achievement, each with an explicit machine-checkable satisfaction/violation condition, excluding static color), and initial facts conditioned on both — enforcing NCP-Bench's **T=0 Absolute Isolation Principle** so the pre-seeded state at "turn 0" never smuggles in future knowledge ([GitHub raw prompts](https://github.com/NLP2CT/NCP-Bench/tree/main/src/ncpbench/prompts)). This structured skeleton, not a narrated recap alone, is what should be loaded into Feature 002's externalized JSON state before real turns begin; the recap prose shown to the model can be generated *from* the skeleton, guaranteeing the two never disagree at seed time. Second, **deliberately seed and check state that changes over the session**, not just static lore: prioritize object/lock-status and possession state (NCP-Bench's #1 case-study failure type), spatial/positional state, character-status flags (alive/dead/present/absent), entity-existence facts (has this NPC/item actually been introduced yet, per the trajectory), and — because the general-memory literature is unambiguous that this is the single most fragile category — any numeric resource that gets revised more than once (HP, gold, inventory counts), tagged explicitly as "updated facts" the way MERIT's evaluation does, since that is the category shown to collapse hardest under both embedding retrieval and naive narration. Third, **calibrate thresholds against the NCP-Bench and RPGBench baselines, not against a from-scratch guess**: expect fact-conflict rates in the double digits even in a well-behaved run (published ranges span 40-68% for episodes that do fail), expect the first conflict substantially before turn 20-30 for most current models under adversarial pressure, and — critically — separate "survival" (no detected contradiction) from "success" (state and narration both correct *and* the story's tracked commitments/quests actually advanced) as two distinct, reported metrics, mirroring NCP-Bench's own Conflict/Survival/Success three-way outcome and mirroring RPGBench's finding that mechanical score and narrative progress can move independently. Fourth, **adopt the cheap, low-risk mitigation layers now and defer hierarchical summarization**: keep a full, un-compressed sliding window of the most recent turns, route all state mutation through the existing real tool calls (`get_state`/`update_state`) rather than any embedding-based memory, and add a second-pass LLM auditor — extending Feature 001's existing narration-vs-state judge — that checks each new narrator turn against the pre-seeded fact ledger and commitments before accepting it, since that class of intervention produced the largest quantified win found anywhere in this research (64.78% hallucination reduction) and requires no architectural change to what Feature 001 already validated. Do not adopt hierarchical/summarization-based memory compression as a default for this feature; its only benchmarked instance nearly tripled a different failure class (player-input conflicts) while barely moving the dominant one (fact conflicts stayed at 59% vs. 60%), and its own authors call the tradeoff unresolved. Fifth, **shape Feature 002's success criteria to report a conflict-rate breakdown by category, not a single pass/fail bar**: following the pattern of Feature 001's SC-002 (report the measured rate as the deliverable insight, not solely a gate), Feature 002 should report per-category conflict counts (fact/commitment/player-input, further split by which of the seeded state categories — locks/possession, position, status, existence, numeric-updated-resource — a given fact conflict falls under), the turn number and normalized position at first conflict, and both a survival rate and a stricter success rate at fixed checkpoints (e.g., turn 20, 50, 80) rather than only at session end — this gives the project the same kind of turn-resolved degradation curve NCP-Bench and RPGBench use, scaled to Feature 002's own tool-calling architecture, and turns "did it break" into "where, on what kind of fact, and how fast."

## Conclusion

The literature converges on a picture that should reassure the project about its proposed test design while sharpening what it measures: synthetic scenario construction is not a lesser substitute for real long-horizon testing, it is how the field's own flagship benchmarks are built, and the actual bar for rigor is isolation discipline during construction (no future-knowledge leakage) plus faithful coverage of the fact categories shown empirically to fail — changing numeric/positional/status state above all, ahead of static lore. What the research does not offer is false comfort about outcomes: even models purpose-tested on exactly this problem break far short of 100 turns, structured/enforced output formats reduce but do not eliminate drift, and the one architectural fix tried at scale traded one failure mode for another rather than solving the underlying problem. That argues for treating Feature 002 not as a pass/fail gate but, like Feature 001 before it, as an instrument for producing a precise, turn-resolved, category-resolved measurement of where this specific architecture's tool-calling and externalized-state design sits on a degradation curve the rest of the field has already shown is steep — a genuinely useful and currently unanswered question, since no source found here has run this comparison (real tool calls vs. narrative-embedded JSON vs. pure prose memory) under matched conditions.
